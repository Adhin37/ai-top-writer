Kuro." — because that is the only protest available to a man whose whole function is writing down what was said.
- **Captain Shuren** — walk down the bluff herself, in the dark, with nothing to gain but one data point, to stand under a dock lamp and watch the lamp girl light it. And carry a pre-cut hawk slip up the hill before the refusal has happened. She tests by arranging to be *present*, never by asking.
- **Commander Ishen** — put two fingers on a barrel lid and give a girl both answers at once: "My grandfather never burned a field he meant to tax. But a little fire now is better than the fort's soldiers, isn't it?" Only Ishen hands over his own people while being sincerely proud of them: "Steady, Kesa."
- **Sergeant Dao** — hear a lie he knows is a lie and let it stand, with a one-word blessing: "Nothing's good." His hands never leave his sleeves; the book earns that by showing us why (a flame "the colour of ash, that lay down and died").
- **Gao** — spend his season's ration of words on a wall rather than a person, and thank his niece in front of the entire garrison with four words that look like a weather report: "Hua. Third step's wet."
- **Yan** — press her thumb into the join between two stones until "the nail went white and the cords stood up in her wrist," and then *run* a warning up five terraces rather than hand it on, leaving the water gate open behind her. She is the one person here who does something with her whole body.
- **Renshu** — arrive before dawn, uninvited, to stand where an officer will see him first; then shout "Earthbender!" at the sky because a twelve-year-old saving spilled rice is the first thing in his life he's had to show anyone.
- **The boy** — go flat on the stones to catch a week's rice "the way you put a hand out when someone drops a bowl." Weakest of the set is **Gao**, who is close to a device (the Man of Five Words); he works, but he is thin next to Bai and Kuro.

## 6. Can I price the threat?

Yes, better than almost anything I've read lately. The fine: "Fifty coppers, the fine was, or a night in the cells: five days hauling sacks on this dock, and weeks of rice for a terrace family." Rice: "fifteen for a measure of rice, crossed out, and twenty-five written beside it in a harder hand" — and twenty-five is "a family's week." The tax in human terms, from Gao: "Five in ten's the seed rice... Nobody plants after." Fish at two, three, four coppers; a skewer at one; thirty coppers paid out of a knot worried loose with the teeth. Her reward is priced too: an extra ration is "a real thing, the kind of thing her mother would weigh in her palm and put away without a word."

I could draw the place: forty lamps, docks to bluff, ten strides apart; the market street climbing past the cooper's and the tea house to the well "where the cobbles turned to steps"; nine terraces, walls above her head, paths "the top of a channel wall a foot wide, with water on one side and paddy on the other."

And I know exactly what losing costs: nine terraces burned, elders hanged from granary beams, or — the version that actually happens — one boy down a river nobody comes back up. "All my years on this river. Never seen one come back up it."

## 7. Where did the biggest thing happen?

On the page, and the one thing wisely kept off it is kept off it well. The arrest, the refusal, and the chained walk down the lit street are all dramatized in real time. The atrocity that gives the threat its weight is delivered as overheard report — "Ash came down the river a week. Grey on the water... My mother skimmed it off the rice pot" — which is the right call; shown, it would be a different and lesser book.

One structural caveat: **the central threatened event cannot happen for three months.** Ch03 puts the brake on deliberately and beautifully ("there was nothing here for fire to hold on to: too much water in every grain"), then substitutes two nearer clocks (the ledger line, the boy). It works, but the headline threat is now a long way off, and I noticed.

## 8. Scenes, and the repeating move

**Ch01 Lit Late** — (a) Kesa + patrol + the boy, dock at dusk, slow walking tempo; (b) the captain lands, one held beat; (c) Kesa + Bai + terrace women + the young private, market stall, busy and comic; (d) the gate: two soldiers, the Avatar joke, then Kuro, quick; (e) Kesa in the dark by the stores shed, overhearing Ishen + Shuren on the veranda — the chapter's engine, very slow; (f) three steps up into the light.

**Ch02 The Garrison's Girl** — (a) dawn stores shed, Kesa + Kuro + the opened tenth crate, quiet dread; (b) the gate, Dao, short and loaded; (c) the stall, Kesa tells Bai, slow; (d) the climb, mud to the shin; (e) Gao's hut: Gao, Yan, three elders, the noon hearth fire, the thatch — the set piece; (f) back down, the dock, Shuren under the first lamp.

**Ch03 One Breath** — (a) pre-dawn yard, Renshu popping flames; (b) the candle test: Renshu's blast, Kesa's deliberate failure, her retry, ten flames; (c) the held flame + the fort speech + the green rice head that will not burn; (d) the stores shed: extra ration, and her name chalked on the crate list.

**Ch04 Five in Ten** — (a) the bluff road, father and daughter counting steps; (b) the assessment, bottom to top, eight marks, rising tempo and crowd; (c) the water gate, ten breaths to Yan; (d) the ninth step: refusal, Renshu's shove, the channel lip that holds, "Kuro. Kuro.", the hawk slip; (e) the walk down, "Kesa, or Hua?"

**Ch05 Downriver in Chains** — (a) the stall on market day, Bai haggling, the overpaid change; (b) the well steps: boot, split sack, two stones, "Earthbender!"; (c) the garrison yard, Renshu performing, `[irons, 1]` on the slate, Dao at the gate; (d) the lit march: no gap, the mother's hand, the captain reading the lamps and finding nothing; (e) the top lamp, the bow lantern going downstream, a woman sitting down on the landing steps; (f) the gate lamp, "Never seen one come back up it."

**The move that repeats:** *a sentence stopped short and handed to someone else to finish.* It is the book's signature and it runs across every mouth. Dao: "Girl standing in the dark that long, she'd have heard—" / "He left it there, for her to finish." Dao again: "He left the rest for her." / "She finished it." Shuren, interrupted by Kesa's own flame leaning: "they hang the elders on the—". Kesa herself, twice: "She did not finish it. She looked at the fish." And Ishen: "He did not say yes. He did not say no. He had said both things, and left them on the barrel for her to choose between." Paired with it is the *said-it-twice* tell, which the book uses as its load-bearing signal for fear — Kuro's "Head down, Kesa," his "Said, I write," his own name, and Shuren asking for that name twice.

The second repeating move is the whole engine: **reading a person off a physical trace.** Boots ("the older one scuffed his left heel"), mud on shins and glove welts, chalk smeared soft from a night under an arm, a flame that leans once. It's used so consistently that when the captain reads the lamps in ch05 and "found nothing," I felt the relief in my chest.

## 9. The best beat

Chapter 4, the bluff road:

> He was standing on twenty-nine, one foot lifted, perfectly still.
> "Twenty-nine," he said.
> She stood on thirty with the captain's book in her arms and waited. He stood on twenty-nine. A grown man, a soldier of the garrison, keeper of its stores, stood on a step in the dark with one foot in the air so that his daughter would be wrong.
> Kesa said nothing at all.
> He put the foot down on thirty. "Thirty," he said, as if it had been his idea.

It works because it is the only tenderness this man has access to, it is funny, and it is the chapter's whole thesis in a joke: Kuro will not be moved off the number, and that is simultaneously the best and the worst thing about him — two hours later the same inflexibility writes "Refused, before the assessing officer" in his daughter's uncle's name. And the payoff is held all the way to the last line of the chapter: "for the first time Kesa could remember, she did not say a single number before he did."

Runner-up, for sheer craft: Bai counting ten coppers back on thirty, with "a fish skewer wrapped in a leaf laid on top of the coins and a few words underneath, low," and Kesa mis-reading it as pity — "She kept the number, though. She did not know why. Five." That is a reader handed a loaded gun and told it's a candlestick.

# VERDICT: 4 / 5

Would follow the serial. The prose is controlled, the economy is real, every object pays (the wick, the chalk mark, the glove, the scorched cuff, the two raised stones nobody dares push back down), and the central conceit — that the skill which made her beloved is precisely the skill that makes her usable — is a premise converted into plot rather than decorated with one. What keeps it off 4.5 is the engine of complicity running five chapters without the protagonist getting a lever, plus the headline threat parked three months out.

## Ranked cost list (biggest cost first)

1. **The protagonist has no lever yet, and ch05 ends on her choosing stillness.** Her one self-authored act in five chapters is "She lit the cooper's lamp on its breath" — the deliberate *absence* of a gap. Thematically exact, dramatically thin: the last chapter's climax is a thing that doesn't happen. "She did not finish it. She looked at the fish." I believe the book knows this; I am not yet sure it has a next gear.
2. **The clock was wound and then unwound.** "Nothing could burn for three months" is a wonderful beat and a structural self-wound. The replacements (an unsent slip, one boy already gone) are smaller than what was promised in ch01.
3. **The narrator explains what the scene already proved.** The "Lit late" re-explanation in ch05 is the clearest case; "So that was what he was for" and the second-step arithmetic are others. Each costs a little of the trust the dialogue had just earned.
4. **The captain's verbal tic is overdrawn.** It is a fine characterizing device and it stopped being invisible to me around ch03, which is where it is thickest.
5. **Renshu is the one character written from outside.** Insecure bully, laughs alone, shouts for credit. His best moment is silent — the shut fist with "a little smoke... between his knuckles, and then none" — and he is never that good again.
6. **The Avatar material does nothing.** The gate joke ("Could be hiding in the granary" / "Then she can tax him five in ten") is charming and then recurs in ch02 as the same joke. Nothing in five chapters needs it, and it borrows weight the rest of the book is earning honestly on its own. If it is a plant, it is planted too lightly to read as one and too often to read as scenery.
7. **Gao is a device with a very good exit line.** Five-words-a-season is a characterization that cannot develop, and the book spends his one great moment ("Third step's wet") in ch04.

## Three changes that would move it up one point

1. **Give Kesa one irreversible act inside chapter 5, and let it be hers.** She builds the plan on the well steps — the cooper's thatch, the fire kit a pace from her elbow — and correctly discards it because the noon sun would take three roofs. Good. Then give her the *other* move the chapter has already armed and doesn't spend: she is the person who trimmed the bow lantern. "The first flame of the night was the boat's." A lamp girl who can hold a flame at an exact size for an hour can also make a wick that will fail at an hour — in the dark, downriver, where no one can date the choice to this street. Let her do something to that lantern and not tell us what, and chapter 6 inherits a secret instead of a mood.
2. **Cut the "Lit late" gloss in ch05 and the second-step gloss after the captain's speech.** Both are stated twice and the second statement is always the weaker. The line that should carry the march home is already there and should stand alone: "The captain's eyes went down them and found nothing, and came back up, and rested a moment on the lamp girl under the well lamp, and found nothing there either."
3. **Put a nearer deadline on the page before ch05 ends, and make it Shuren's, not the rice's.** Right now the only live date is three months off. One line in her mouth on the well steps — the hawk goes at the next full moon, or she leaves the river in sixty days and means to leave it clean — converts "when the rice is dry" from a season into a countdown, and makes the unsent slip in her glove tick instead of merely sit.

## 1. The story in one sentence

A fifteen-year-old girl who lights the forty curfew lamps of an occupied river town — Fire Nation father, Earth Kingdom mother — is chosen by a visiting homeland captain to burn her own uncle's rice terrace, because the fire the job needs is small, slow and exactly held, and that is the only kind of fire anyone has ever let her learn.

There *is* opposition and there *are* moves. The opposition is one woman, Captain Shuren, who never raises her voice and wins every exchange. Kesa's moves: she trims a wick that doesn't need trimming to buy a boy a dark street (ch1); she carries the captain's own threat up the hill in ten breaths at a channel gate (ch4: *"Fort's three days upriver," she said to the gate. "If he says no, she sends a hawk. All nine steps. And the elders." The water went over the sill. "From the granary beams."*); and in ch5 she plans a rescue and chooses not to do it. That last is the honest shape of the five chapters: the moves get smaller, not bigger. Worth saying plainly.

## 2. The world, explained to a friend

It is the *Avatar: The Last Airbender* world, said so on the page, not inferred: *"One man who bends all four, fire, water, earth and air, and nobody's seen him in a hundred years"*; *"Hundred years of war"*; Fire Nation, Earth Kingdom, forbidden earthbending.

What's different and how it works — all from the page:
- **Fire is licensed.** *"the garrison held every fire bigger than a hearth. A family could cook its supper, and that was all; kilns, forges, field burns and street lamps needed the garrison's leave and a garrison firebender to light them."*
- **The lamps *are* the law.** *"These lamps were the curfew. Once a street was lit, anyone out on it without a garrison token... could be stopped."* Hence the town's blessing, *"May your lamp be lit late: may you be spared."* The whole book is built on this one rule, and it is a genuinely good one.
- **Firebending has physics.** Range is finite (ten paces is Kesa's edge, tested with candles on a wall). The sun feeds it: her father's only rule was *"Never at noon"*, and when she breaks it on an ember, *"The fire jumped... a full hand higher than she had meant, yellow and roaring, and it leaned."* Green rice will not take: *"It hissed... the flame sat on them and went smaller and smaller and would not take."*
- **Earthbending is a capital-ish crime.** *"Anyone caught at it, the garrison takes away."* Paid off in ch5 when the boy saves spilled rice with two stones, and in Dao's *"All my years on this river... Never seen one come back up it."*
- **Tax by hawk.** Three in ten for three generations, raised to five in ten by a messenger hawk, in a short year. The refusal goes in a ledger; the ledger line can be copied onto a slip *"rolled tight... the size of a hawk's leg tube"* and sent to a fort three days upriver whose soldiers *"burn all nine, and they hang the elders on the— ...from the granary beams."*

**Who she is:** Kesa to the garrison (her father's name), Hua to her mother's family. Lamplighter since eleven, hasn't missed a lamp. *"In the market she was the lamp girl in the red tunic. Up on the terraces she was the garrison's girl. Here she was the lamplighter... the one place in Lantern Landing where she was not half of anything."*

**What she wants:** this is the one soft spot in an otherwise fully-furnished world. Her want is negative and mostly unspoken — to stay invisible, to keep the lamps hers (*"She had thought the lamps were the one thing in this town that was hers"*), to not be the hand. The nearest thing to a stated desire is a shameful one, and it is the best line in ch3: *"Let it be him... and let the lamp girl be what he thought she was, someone sent to hold the candles."* She never says what she wants *for* anyone.

**If she fails:** one terrace burns by her hand and the valley knows whose hand; or the refusal goes by hawk and nine terraces burn and the elders hang. What happens to *Kesa personally* if she simply refuses — **not on the page.** The commander deliberately won't say: *"He did not say yes. He did not say no. He had said both things, and left them on the barrel for her to choose between."* Artful, but it means her own price is never quoted, and that costs the book tension (see §6).

## 3. Would I read chapter six?

Yes — because a slip of paper with her uncle's refusal on it is sitting unsent in a glove-smoothing woman's sleeve, and I want to know what makes her send it.

## 4. Where I skimmed; anything that lectured

Two places, honestly.

- **The world-rules paragraph in ch1**, the one beginning *"In Lantern Landing, an Earth Kingdom river town the Fire Nation had occupied for three generations..."* — straight authorial briefing, three sentences of ordinance. It is efficient and I was grateful for it later, but I read it the way you read a sign.
- **The Avatar banter at the gate** (ch1): *"Could be hiding in the granary." / "Maybe the Avatar's in the rice." / "Then she can tax him five in ten."* I skimmed this. It is there to establish the setting's franchise and to misdirect about the captain's errand, and it is repeated almost verbatim in ch2 (*"Come for the Avatar. Everyone at the gate says so"*). Two servings of a joke that is not about anyone in the story.
- **Mild:** the fire-kit inventory in ch2 (*"You soaked the sacking and laid it where you did not want the fire to go. You raked a strip clean ahead of the fire..."*) reads like a manual, but it's a girl reading a crate, and it pays off, so I'll let it stand.
- The thing I genuinely got tired of is **the captain's plan being restated**. *"A slow fire, a creeping one, that a crew can walk beside and steer, so that it takes that one terrace and stops at its walls"* comes back in some form at least four more times, twice inside ch2 alone. By the third I was skipping to the end of the sentence.

## 5. Each named character: what would they do that nobody else would

- **Kesa / Hua** — stop at a lamp that doesn't need her, take out a blade and *"snipped at nothing"* so a stranger gets a dark street; then hold a flame at thumb-size on her open palm for a full hour while a woman describes hanging her relatives, and let it lean exactly once.
- **Bai** (mother) — disarm a soldier by feeding him: *"And while you're standing there, have you eaten?... Belly or tail, then"* — and buy her daughter a bowl's worth of time with it. And: count back **five coppers too many** to a terrace woman and say nothing, ever, about it.
- **Kuro** (father) — stand in the dark on step twenty-nine *"with one foot in the air so that his daughter would be wrong."* Also: scratch a lamp into a barrel lid so no one waters her oil; stop his lips at seven when he hears what noon means.
- **Captain Shuren** — walk down the bluff herself, at dusk, to stand under one lamp and make the lamp girl light it while she watches, *"knowing she was watched."* And hand the girl the threat rather than make it: *"Does the girl need to hear this, Captain?" / "I think she should, Commander, should she not? It is her mother's people."*
- **Commander Ishen** — destroy someone by praising them. *"Never slow. Not once in four years."* Then: *"He had just handed her over with it, and he would go to bed tonight pleased with her."* He does it twice more, including *"Steady, Kesa"* over a boy in irons.
- **Gao** (uncle) — spend five words on a wall instead of on a person: *"Nobody burns what they tax," he said... to the terraces, not to her.* And thank someone with weather: *"Hua," he said. "Third step's wet."*
- **Yan** (cousin) — push her thumb into the join between two stones *"until the nail went white and the cords stood up in her wrist, as if she wanted to push the whole wall somewhere else."* Then run a warning up five terraces and leave the gate she was tending running all day, flooding the third step.
- **Renshu** — arrive before dawn to stand where an officer will see him first, popping a flame in his palm at an empty veranda; then, having missed nine of ten candles, announce *"Got most of them!"*
- **Sergeant Dao** — refuse to finish his own sentences, and let a lie stand on purpose: *"Girl standing in the dark that long, she'd have heard—" He left it there, for her to finish.* / *"Nothing's good," he said.*

Weakest of the nine is **Ishen** — the sentimental hereditary colonial who tells you about his grandfather is a type, and the two-finger tap is doing a lot of work to individuate him. Renshu is thin but is honestly meant to be: a man with no interior being used by someone who has one.

## 6. Can I price the threat?

Better than almost anything I've read in this format. Rice: *"fifteen for a measure of rice, crossed out, and twenty-five written beside it in a harder hand"* — and what that *is*: *"That's a family's week of rice up ten coppers."* The curfew fine: *"Fifty coppers... or a night in the cells: five days hauling sacks on this dock, and weeks of rice for a terrace family."* Fish: belly two, tail three, whole four; a skewer, one copper. Tax: three in ten → five in ten, and what five means — *"Pay five in a short year and we're eating the seed rice by spring" / "And plant what, after?... Air?"*. The worst case has a precedent with a date and a texture: *"Twenty summers back... The fort did that one. Ash came down the river a week. Grey on the water... My mother skimmed it off the rice pot."* And the end of the river: *"His mother still sets him a bowl."*

I could draw the place without hesitating: forty lamps ten strides apart, docks to bluff; the market street climbing past the cooper's and the tea house to a well where the cobbles turn to steps; the granary and the sluice; nine terraces, each path *"the top of a channel wall, water on one side and paddy on the other"*; the garrison gate with the painted roll of commanders, *"the name Ishen... three times."*

**The one unpriced thing is Kesa.** Everyone else's loss is in coppers and bowls of rice. Hers is never quoted by anyone — not the commander, not the captain, not Dao. I don't know whether refusing costs her the pole, the extra ration, her father's job, or irons. For a book this precise about money, that silence reads less like restraint than like a choice not to close the trap.

## 7. Where did the biggest thing happen?

Mostly on the page, with one structural problem.

The biggest *event* in these five chapters — the boy's arrest and the lamplit march to the boat — is wholly on the page, in real time, and it is the best sequence in the book. The captain's plan is delivered in dialogue, but as an overheard scene with the girl standing in the dark, which is a scene, not a report. The fort's precedent is a report (the hen woman on the third step), and that is the right way to deliver it: hearsay from a woman stroking a hen is worse than any description.

The problem is that **the book's own engine gets switched off in a report.** At the end of ch3: *"In my grandfather's day... the cut came after the dry week... Late rains this year. Three months off, that puts it."* The burning — the thing the whole premise promises — is deferred by a line of dialogue to a date beyond the horizon. *"Green rice would not burn... The rice had three months."* The author knows this and immediately substitutes a nearer clock (*"Gao had two days"*), and then in ch5 a third (the boy). It works, chapter to chapter. But across five chapters the main threat has receded while the protagonist's agency has shrunk, and the two together are what keep this off a 5 for me.

## 8. Scenes, and the author's repeating move

**Ch1 (Lit Late)** — dock, dusk, Kesa + boy + patrol, slow and unhurried; the landing, Shuren steps off a boat and reads the gap in the lamps, quick; Bai's stall, dialogue tempo, the fish-skewer stall-for-time; the market street lit bottom-to-top, steady; the garrison gate, Kuro and the crates, brisk; the veranda eavesdrop, near-static, the hinge of the whole thing.

**Ch2 (The Garrison's Girl)** — stores shed at dawn, Kesa reads the crates, slow; the gate, Dao, three exchanges and out; Bai's stall, the brown jacket; the climb, Kesa's leg goes in to the shin, comic then not; Gao's hearth, the elders, the fire jumps — fastest beat in the book; Yan's accusation, a standoff; the dock at dusk, Shuren's order, cold and short.

**Ch3 (One Breath)** — pre-dawn yard, Renshu popping his flame, waiting; the candle test, ten paces, two rounds; the held flame + the fort threat, an hour compressed to a page, the book's best-controlled scene; the green rice head, a beat; the stores shed, extra ration and her name chalked under *beaters*.

**Ch4 (Five in Ten)** — the bluff road, father and daughter counting steps, intimate and slow; steps one to four, procedural, marks accumulating; the fifth step, Yan at the channel gate, ten breaths, the one thing Kesa does; the ninth step, the refusal, Renshu's shove, the lip that holds like stone — highest pressure; the descent, *"Kesa, or Hua?"*, slow and awful.

**Ch5 (Downriver in Chains)** — market day at the stall, fast dialogue, gutting fish, five coppers; the well steps, the boot, the rice, the two stones, the arrest — the peak; the garrison yard, Renshu telling it, Dao at the gate; the lighting of the street against the procession, processional tempo, the best thing in the five chapters; the gate lamp and the river.

**The move that repeats:** every character is given one small physical habit, and the habit is then used as the whole instrument. Ishen's *"two fingers"* tapping a rail, a buckle, a barrel lid, a pole. Shuren's gloves, *"finger by finger."* Dao's hands going further into his sleeves. Yan's thumb in a wall. Gao's palm flat on stone. Kuro's lips moving on a count. Bai weighing a single copper in her palm *"a long time for one copper."* When the author wants a reaction, he moves the tic, never the face — *"The fingers stopped on the rail."* It is disciplined and it mostly works.

Across mouths, the signature is **the same words coming back from the other side**: the commander's *"you don't burn a field you mean to tax"* returns twenty pages later as Gao's *"Nobody burns what they tax"*; *"at the cut"* leaves Kesa's mouth at a channel gate and comes out of Gao's in front of the captain with *"three"* in front of it instead of five; *"Lit late"* is a blessing in ch1 and a death sentence in ch5. Second signature: **someone destroys someone else by telling the truth they were asked for** — Ishen's *"Never slow"*, Kuro's *"Forty dips, Captain... Never short"*, Kuro's *"Kuro, Captain. Kuro."*

## 9. The best beat

> He was standing on twenty-nine, one foot lifted, perfectly still.
>
> "Twenty-nine," he said.
>
> She stood on thirty with the captain's book in her arms and waited. He stood on twenty-nine. A grown man, a soldier of the garrison, keeper of its stores, stood on a step in the dark with one foot in the air so that his daughter would be wrong.
>
> Kesa said nothing at all.
>
> He put the foot down on thirty. "Thirty," he said, as if it had been his idea.
>
> "Thirty," she agreed, and he did not smile, quite, and they went on down.

It works because it is the only love scene the man is capable of, it is funny, and it costs nothing — and then it is the setup for two later devastations in the same register: the lips stopping at seven when he hears *"At midday, I think"*, and *"Kuro, Captain," her father said. "Kuro." / He said it twice.* A character who can only express himself by counting, in a book where everything is counted, is the kind of structural idea that makes the rest look easy.

Runner-up, and nearly as good: Bai counting back ten coppers when the change was five, and *"She kept the number, though. She did not know why. Five."* The author declines to explain it, Kesa supplies a wrong explanation (pity), and the number is left on the page for later. That is confidence.

## Verdict

**4 — I would follow this serial.**

It knows what its world costs to the copper, its central rule (lamps are the curfew) is doing real work in every chapter, and the final sequence — a girl lighting a boy's way into a prison boat, perfectly, one breath a lamp, because any gap she leaves will be read — is the kind of scene a serial earns its subscribers with. What's keeping it off 5 isn't taste; it's two things I think would actually stop me somewhere past chapter ten.
