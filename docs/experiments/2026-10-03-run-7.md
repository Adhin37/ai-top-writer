## Seed, and why this one

> In this river city houses are grown, not built: plant a seed-stone, feed it lime and river silt,
> and in a year it rises into walls and rooms. A house must be fed all its life. A starving house
> shrinks: doors narrow, rooms seal, and an old one can close on whoever is still inside. Rent is
> paid in feed, and one guild owns the silt. A young assessor whose job is to certify starving
> houses as lost, so the guild can clear and replant the plot, finds his own family's house on his
> list, though they have never missed a feeding. Someone is cutting the feed sold in the poor
> quarter, and buying every plot that dies. For Royal Road readers who like a clever main
> character, clear rules and a slow climb.

- **Away from what the room has seen.** The slice novel is roads that shift each winter, surveyed
  by an apprentice. Plan 04's throwaway is oaths sworn on bells, mended by an apprentice. The
  knowledge bases' worlds are a sealed habitat where air is billed and a harbour town that rows
  out its drowned. A grep of `kb/` for grown or fed houses finds nothing.
- **Away from the defaults lesson 26 found.** The seed names no one: not the city, the guild, the
  protagonist or his family. Every name is the planner's, and the leak sweep at the end of `/new`
  checks them against the examples. The protagonist is not an apprentice, and not a gifted
  outsider: he works for the system that is starving his family.
- **It suits a webnovel-clear chapter 1.** The central rule is a mechanism with a cost a reader
  can see in the street (a house that shrinks, and can close on the people inside), and it can be
  said in one plain sentence.
- **Royal Road shape.** A clever protagonist, a mystery with a clock (his family's house), a
  person to find, and readers' tastes named instead of an author to imitate, so round 5 has to do
  its job.

  and squeezed. One viewpoint, close third, past tense. He is twenty, a junior assessor two years
  in: methodical, good with numbers and patterns, bad at saying no to people above him. He believes

  neutral. **No chosen-one gift.** His edge is something he knows how to do that most people don't;
  if it is a gift or a forbidden technique, **its cost must come from how houses work**, not
  nosebleeds, headaches or fainting. He is not reborn and knows nothing of the future. Modules:
  mystery clues on; romance in the background, slow, never the plot. Everything else, the
  recommendation.

  and sells feed on credit; a house is most families' whole wealth. A house that dies goes back to
  the guild's register, and its plot is sold for replanting. In the poor quarter the houses are old
  and thin. People talk to their houses; a house is family. One city. Keep it small.

  senior assessor he trusts, who may or may not be in on it. Romance in the background, slow.

# Verdict: 4.5

I would follow this serial, and I'd recommend it to the right person with one caveat about pacing. The prose is controlled and never ornamental, the central rule is concrete and *priced* in two currencies (pence and sacks), the chapter 5 set piece is genuinely harrowing and arrives after four chapters of earned restraint, and the book's thesis — that a perfect record of doing everything right is exactly what kills you, and that the instrument certifying you safe is calibrated *"on the advice of the Wardens' Guild"* — is dramatized, not stated. What keeps it off 5 is that the plot machinery is thinner than the world: one convenient ally, one legible villain, and a protagonist who at the end of five chapters is holding the same fingernail he was holding at the end of three.

## Ranked cost list

1. **Pell's windowsill is a gift.** The investigation's single biggest step — "grey out of the sack", "I mix it black... He walks in with a cord and walks out with the answer.
2. **Aurel Vane is priced by the middle of chapter 2.** Every single thing he does is charm with a lien on it — the barley sugar, the name remembered, the dead husband invoked, the four sacks written against Iven's wage, and the pre-emptive *"I'm sorry about the house, Iven."* He is never once simply right, or simply kind, or wrong-footed. The chapter's closing hook — *"How did Aurel Vane know?"* — therefore lands as confirmation rather than question. Note the contrast: the author trusts me to find Prent and Hob complicated and does not trust me with Aurel.
3. **The Sallow house does not move.** Ch5's last page makes a deliberate virtue of this — *"the same fingernail as the night before last"* — and I admire the nerve. But across five chapters the only forward clock a reader can see is a date on a form, and the one physical symptom (a sticking pantry door, since spring) is in place before chapter 1 ends. The knot-count is invisible to everyone but Iven and is, by his own admission, *"a rough one."*
4. **The gesture motif is over-poured.** Hand-on-wall / pat-the-wall appears, by my rough count, more than fifteen times across five chapters, and in ch1 alone Marra does it at lines 155, 173, 183 and 255. 5. **The narrator occasionally argues.** *"and the Shallows hated it for that, and the Shallows was wrong."* Also *"That was the rest of it. The Wardens' Guild owned..."* These are the only sentences in five chapters where I could hear an author, and they are doing work the scenes already do better.

The clearance-file reveal is at risk of being the chapter's whole payoff while also being the reader's opening hypothesis.
7. **Three named characters are functions.** Mother Dunn (one line, one open door), Bram Tull (menace and a padlock), Tobin Fell (a nib and a volume problem). Fine for now; two of the three are load-bearing for later and will need to be people when they get there.

2. **Give Aurel one scene where he is right and Iven is wrong, at a price.** He has one already, half-built — the charter argument with Marra, where *"both of them believed it, and that neither of them had ever needed to say it any louder than this"* is the best sentence about him in the book. ## 1. The story in one sentence

The city inspector whose seal condemns starving houses discovers that his own family's house — fed four sacks every seventh day for a hundred years, with a wall of chalk fours to prove it — is starving anyway because the feed itself has been cut, and he has ten days to find proof he can put in front of anybody before he must sign a form about the house his sister sleeps in.

There is opposition (the guild that sells every sack, a Warden who knew about the house before Iven told anyone, an office whose own form has no box for the one measurement that works) and there are moves already made: he signed the Dennet clearing order; he lied to his mother's face — *"Every door inside the Line." He shut the survey book. "It went well."*; he used *"Rule twelve, clause three, Senior Prent"* to buy himself a week against his own house; he put his secret instrument into a stranger's hand to recruit her.

## 2. The world, to a friend

In Vellmouth *"a house was grown, not built, and a house ate all its life."* Each house grows from a heart-stone set in the kitchen floor, *"broad as a barrel-head,"* and every seventh day — feed day — the household pours lime-and-river-silt onto it, one sack per room, naming the rooms aloud as they pour: *"Front room," Marra said, to the house.* The stone drinks it: *"It sank, the way water sinks into dry sand."*

Underfed, a house eats itself, and in a fixed order, which is the rule the whole plot runs on: *"Back rooms first, then the doorways, then the ceilings: a house fed too little fed on itself, from its far end in toward its heart, and always in that order, so the order told you how far it had gone."* Old houses hide it: *"An old house held its shape for years, hiding what it had eaten of itself, and then one night it came in on itself, walls to the middle in a few hours, on whoever was still inside."* The hours of warning are called singing.

The Wardens' Guild owns the silt beds and sells all feed; a house may buy only at its own quarter's yard, on credit written against its name in the yard's book, and the Shallows has one yard with one gate. The Survey Office measures doorways with a brass rule against *"[The Lost Line, as drawn on the advice of the Wardens' Guild, with a margin for safety.]"* — note who draws the line — and certifies houses lost; ten days to empty, then clearers lift the heart-stone, the plot reverts to the guild register and is sold for replanting, and the family goes to the board-shacks: *"houses nailed together out of planks, built and not grown, dead from the day they were put up."*

Protagonist: Iven Sallow, twenty, two years in the grey coat, Shallows-born, a reader of columns (*"looking for gaps was what his eyes did"*). He wants his own survey to come out honest — and when it comes out clean and the house is still starving, he wants to know why. What happens if he fails is laid out in both directions and neither is survivable: *"If he certified it lost, the clearers would come for the stone, and the Sallows would go down to the boards by the dredge cuts. If he wrote anything else, he would be writing it about a starving house, with his sister asleep in it."* Chapter 5 shows exactly what the second option looks like.

Inferred: that Aurel Vane is behind the adulteration and that "E. Barrow" is his instrument **(guess)** — the page gives only *"Never heard of a Barrow"* and nine Shallows plots to one name. Why anyone wants unplantable Shallows ground: **not on the page**. Who wrote the sealed report on the Sallow house: **not on the page** — explicitly withheld by rule nine. What Marra's dredging wage is: **not on the page** (only that feed costs her 16 pence a week and the hall is paying *"Third week, not second"*).

## 3. Would I read chapter six?

Yes, tonight. Not for the conspiracy — for the pantry door. Iven has eight days, a measurement with no box on the form, and a sister sleeping next to the room that goes first, and I want to know what he writes.

## 4. Skimming and lectures

Very little skimming. The one place I genuinely slid was the clearance-file mechanics in ch03: *"The clearance file was a long book ruled across both pages into columns... And at the far edge of the right-hand page a last column for the buyer, headed in a clerk's copperplate, which stayed empty until the guild's slip came back after a sale."* Three columns of office furniture before the thing I was being set up for. The date arithmetic around it (*"the next one fell thirty-nine days from today. He counted it out on his fingers under the desk"*) I skipped and did not miss.

Closest to lecture, all short and all in the narrator's mouth rather than a character's: ch01's *"Certification as lost: the office's seal on a form that said a house had starved past saving, and must be emptied within ten days and cleared. It was what the Survey Office was for."* — and ch02's *"Nobody in that queue owed pence. They owed sacks, because a sack was what a house ate, one room for one week, and so the yard counted debt the way a house would have counted it."* The second one earns itself with the next clause (*"every woman in the queue knew her own line in sacks the way she knew her children's ages"*), but both are the author stepping to the front of the stage.

One flat restatement: ch01 already gave me *"By law a house bought its feed only at its own quarter's yard, written against its name in the yard's book, and the Shallows had one yard,"* and ch02 gives it again — *"A house bought its feed by register, at its own quarter's yard and nowhere else, so for the Shallows there was nowhere else."*

## 5. What each named character would do that nobody else would

- **Iven Sallow** — times a house's breathing with a knotted string he has never told his office about, because *"The office had a rule for everything, and no rule for this, and he had never seen a reason to give them one"* — then weaponizes a procedural clause against his own employer to buy a week. Nobody else in this story invents an instrument and then hides it from the people who would benefit.
- **Marra Sallow** — addresses the building as the responsible adult in the room. In a kitchen with the ceiling coming down she does not plead with Abel; she speaks past him: *"'Let him go, then,' she told the house."* And she can praise and shame her son in one breath in front of a queue: *"'Best wage on the row.' Then she looked at Iven, at the grey coat... 'And he's in that for it.'"*
- **Wren** — writes the number of days to a seed-stone *"small, low down by the doorframe, where Marra would have to be on her knees to see it,"* and audits the yard's arithmetic out loud at the counter (*"Thirty-two," Wren said round the barley sugar, before he could*). She is the only person who answers a disaster with a question: *"So how does a fed house sing?"*
- **Odile Prent** — rests her hand on a sealed report she could open and will not, and has never wanted to: *"It came to Iven that Odile Prent had never in her life needed to open a report early, and he would have bet a week's wage she had never wanted to."* Her reprimand is three words — *"Second bell, Sallow"* — and she squares papers that *"did not need it."*
- **Aurel Vane** — crouches on a yard floor so his good coat-tails touch the boards, pays a child a barley sugar *"For checking my sums. Nobody else in this yard will,"* and converts a gift into a lien while you watch: *"I'm not giving you anything, Marra. I'm lending to a house that pays."* Then condoles for a house nobody has told him about.
- **Bram Tull** — stops a song *"in the middle of a word"* at the sight of a coat, and answers a question about lime by walking away to stand with his back against the one locked door in the yard.
- **Pell Arnow** — keeps evidence nobody asked her for: three pickle jars corked wet the morning she poured and sealed over with candle wax, *"Not every sack. Just the ones that came light in my arms."* And she knows exactly what they're worth: *"They're my word. That's all they are. My word in a jar."*
- **Hob Dennet** — says goodbye to his house as to a spouse — *"Well... Goodbye, then, old girl. You did your best."* — and then, a week later, takes a child out of the arms of the man who condemned it, in silence: *"He did not speak. He did not look at Iven."*
- **Abel Orrin** — talks his own house through its death and refuses rescue on the grounds that he is the one doing the keeping: *"'It's not keeping me,' Abel said. 'I'm keeping it.'"*
- **Tobin Fell** — licks his nib before every line and says a listed house out loud across the office *"at the pitch he said everything, which was for the room."*
- **Tansy Orrin** — hours after the house kills her grandfather, defends it with arithmetic on her fingers: *"'Three,' she said, to nobody. 'We gave it three. Every feed day.' A finger, a finger, a finger. 'I counted.'"*
- **Garret Orrin** — asks two questions and doesn't wait: *"Was it on your list?" ... "Then what's your list for?"*
- **Mother Dunn** — greets a grey coat with *"Is it us?"* and will not say goodnight back.
- **The clearers' foreman** — says everything to his horse, including the thesis of the whole serial.

## 6. Pricing the threat

Yes, better than almost anything I read. A sack is 4 pence; four rooms is 16 pence a week (*"Sixteen pence a week, my lady, for your dinner"*); eight sacks owed is 32; Iven earns *"Twenty pence a day"*, which is *"eight pence more than the docks paid"*; a seed-stone is *"Three thousand pence"* = *"A hundred and fifty days... If you never ate"*; a cleared plot fetches *"Twelve hundred pence... A hundred days on the docks"*, so nine of them is *"Nine hundred days on the docks"* for ground nobody plants. Debt is denominated in the only unit that matters: *"A family three sacks behind was three weeks from a narrowing door."*

I could draw the yard from the page — the barges at the cut, baskets of black silt going *"grey the moment it touched the yard,"* hip-high vats under a lean-to, a grey dog under a plank-and-trestle counter with scale at one end and book at the other, and one shed with *"a door that shut"* and a finger-long key on the foreman's belt. I could draw the office: two rows of desks, Prent's at the far end under the window *"so that she could see all of them and none of them could see her paper."*

Not priced, deliberately: what a Shallows plot is worth to the buyer, and who E. Barrow is.

## 7. Where the biggest thing happened

On the page, at length, and it is the right thing. Chapter 5 dramatizes a house closing on a man over hours: the arms through the narrowing gap — *"he felt the skin go off the outsides of both his forearms like bark off a stick"* — Tansy coming through *"a shoulder, a ribcage, a hip"*, the doorway shutting with *"a sound like a mouth closing"*, and the walls meeting as *"a sound like somebody who has been holding their breath a long time finally letting it out."* Chapter 1 likewise puts the Dennet heart-stone coming up on the page (*"a sound like a boot coming out of deep mud"*) rather than in summary.

The one event that is only ever reported is the one that defines Iven: the grandmother on the Steps who *"came out of it sideways... with her wedding plate held flat to her chest."* That is a choice, and a good one — the whole point is that the street owns the story now: *"The Steps had taken it off him... and made a story out of it, and the story had his name in it."*

## 8. The scenes, and the move that repeats

**ch01** — (i) Tanner's Row plot 7, dawn: Iven signs the clearing order and walks the dead house; stone lifted; Hob watching from across the street. Measured, cold. (ii) Survey Office: Prent hands him the list; last line is his mother's house. Fast, three pages of pressure in one page. (iii) Eight houses, summary tempo, one paragraph. (iv) Sallow kitchen, feed day: the pour, the chalk four, the cord, the grey pinch. Slow, domestic, ends on discovery.
**ch02** — (i) Shallows yard, morning: the vats, Bram and the locked shed, Pell's shovel, the two pinches. (ii) The counter: Marra pays 32, Aurel lends four sacks against Iven's wage. (iii) The gate: Aurel's condolence for a house nobody has mentioned. One continuous rising scene, dialogue tempo.
**ch03** — (i) Office: rule nine and the sealed report under Prent's palm; Tobin's outburst. (ii) Two houses, summary. (iii) Sallow kitchen: he surveys his own home while Wren refuses to ask, and lies to Marra. Slow and awful. (iv) Office, next morning: *"Clean figures, Sallow,"* the bought week, then the clearance ledger and nine lines of E. Barrow. Accelerating.
**ch04** — (i) Yard gate, dusk: Pell refuses twice. (ii) The dead plot by the cuts: the cord shown, the price named. (iii) Mother Dunn's house, Pell's room: three jars. Two-hander, slow, quiet.
**ch05** — (i) Sallow kitchen, small hours: the singing starts and is not his house. (ii) The Orrin house: the long set-piece, hours, episodic by design (*"He knew it in pieces"*). (iii) Grey morning: Garret's question, Tansy's fingers. (iv) Walk home with Wren; the pantry door remeasured. Falling, then one cold figure.

**The move that repeats — a hand laid flat on a surface, and a count.** It is in every chapter and in every mouth, and it is the book's whole argument: that these people keep faultless records and the records are worthless. Marra's palm and *"You've been good"*; Iven's palm and sixty knots; Wren's chalk fours *"every four the same size, every column ruled straight"*; Abel's doorframe *"worn pale where his hand lay on it"* and then his hand on the stone; Tansy's fingers folding down; Pell's *"lips moved on the third"* shovel of silt and her three jars *"squared... in a row with one finger, the way his Senior squared papers"*; Prent squaring forms corner to corner; Bram's grey finger down the page; Aurel *"Two hundred and eight, I make it. Am I right?"* and the pinch rubbed between finger and thumb (Aurel does it on the scale pan, Iven on his own kitchen floor, Pell across the back of his hand). The chapter endings are the same move: ch01 closes on *"every one of them was correct,"* ch05 on *"a fingernail narrower than its frame: the same fingernail as the night before last."*

A second signature, just as consistent: **the hardest lines are addressed to an object or an animal.** The foreman tells his horse; Marra tells the house; Abel tells the stone; Marra pays *"Never behind," she told the book*; Bram says *"Sallow," ... to the book*; Marra says *"Did he," ... to the onions.* Nobody in the Shallows says the important thing to a face.

## 9. The best beat

Chapter 5, in the Orrin kitchen with the ceiling going:

> "Abel." Short, and plain. A dredger's daughter to an old man off the next row. "Tansy's out. She wants you. Come on."
> Abel looked up. Not at Iven. At Marra.
> "Evening, Marra," he said, as if she had stopped at his step on her way up from the beds.
> Marra's mouth went tight. She looked at the stone, at the old man's hand on it, and she spoke past him, to the walls, the way she spoke to her own.
> "Let him go, then," she told the house.
> "It's not keeping me," Abel said. "I'm keeping it."

It works because the world's one odd habit — talking to the house — has been built for five chapters as comedy and tenderness, and here it pays as the only grammar available for a man choosing to die, and as the only argument available to the woman trying to stop him. Marra addresses the house because that is where she believes the agency is; Abel corrects her on exactly that point, and in correcting her he refuses the board-shacks, refuses his son, and tells us what eighty years of feeding something buys you. No one explains any of it. And *"Evening, Marra"* is the line I'll remember.

Runner-up, for the opposite reason — it is the cheapest possible sentence and it lands like a dropped stone: *"'Was it on your list?' ... 'Then what's your list for?'"*

## Verdict: **4.5**

I would follow this serial without being asked twice, and I would hand it to a particular kind of friend unprompted. What keeps it off a flat 5 for me is not craft but two things I could feel the author deciding: the villain is legible early, and the prose holds one temperature for five chapters.

### Ranked cost list, biggest first

1. **Aurel Vane is solved too soon.** Chapter 2 flies four flags in one scene — the one locked door in an open yard with the foreman standing in front of it, the too-smooth explanation of why loose feed goes light (*"That's the whole secret, and Bram will sing it to you for a penny"*), the loan written against Iven's wage, the licence dangled over Pell — and then closes on *"I'm sorry about the house, Iven."* After that the reader is no longer investigating, only waiting. The best suspicion in these pages is Iven's honest one — *"He noted it the way he'd note a column that added up but had a thumbprint on it"* — and the chapter doesn't trust it to carry alone.
2. **The refrain is rung too often.** *"Old ones hold their shape. Hold it and hold it, and you'd never know. Then they go all at once"* appears four times across five chapters. By its last use — Iven handing it to Wren as a lie — I was admiring the architecture instead of feeling the betrayal, because I had already heard it twice in narration. Same for *"broad as a barrel-head"* (four), *"the ribs of a sleeping dog"* / *"faint as a sleeper's ribs"* (three), and the wall-patting (eight-plus).
3. **Pell's cooperation turns inside one chapter, on an elision.** She refuses twice, hard and well-reasoned (*"There's a licence on Master Vane's desk with my name on it"*), and then has, already waiting on her windowsill, exactly the evidence set the plot needs. Her reason for starting is withheld rather than given: *"I've been doing it since—" She stopped, and did not finish that.* That is a withhold standing where a motive should be, and it is the one place the machinery shows.
4. **One uniform register.** Everything is understated at the same pitch, which is the book's great strength in ch01–ch04 and a small cost in ch05, where the house-closing is narrated in the same dry cadence as a door width. *"He did not know afterwards how long the next part was. He knew it in pieces"* is a lovely device, but it also lets the author keep the control he has had all along at the one moment the prose could have lost it.
5. **A little duplicated exposition** — the buy-at-your-own-yard rule laid out twice (ch01, ch02), and the clearance-ledger columns itemised before they pay off.
6. **The protagonist has not yet paid institutionally.** He has lied to his mother and bought a week, but nothing has been taken from him by the office or the guild. Garret's *"Then what's your list for?"* is the first real bill, and it arrives on the last page of five.

### Three changes that would move it up one point

1. **Defuse Aurel in ch02 and let the reader rebuild him.** Keep *"I'm sorry about the house, Iven"* — it is the hook — but give it a plausible innocent channel on the page in the same chapter rather than in Iven's speculation afterwards: let Tobin Fell be visible at the bridges, or let a carter mention the list, so that the yard's knowledge is explained and the suspicion has to be re-earned by the jars and the ledger. And move the locked shed out of chapter 2 entirely; Bram's key is enough on its own, and the shed reads as a sign saying EVIDENCE HERE.
2. **Give Pell a reason instead of a dash.** Replace *"I've been doing it since—" She stopped* with the fact — one sentence about whose house, or which winter, or which sack she carried that was light. Her refusal is the best-argued thing in ch04; her help needs the same ballast, and a stated reason costs nothing and buys her a spine for the next twenty chapters.
3. **Cut the refrain to two uses, first and last.** Leave it in the foreman's mouth to his horse in ch01 and in Iven's mouth to Wren in ch05 — *"Old ones go all at once," he said* — and delete the ch01 reprise (*"The Sallow house was close to a hundred years old. Old ones hold their shape..."*) and the ch03 reprise at the pantry door. Then the last use is the first time Iven has said it, which is what makes it a lie, and Wren's refusal to push — *"she looked back at the road in front of her and let it go"* — becomes unbearable instead of neat.

## 1. The story in one sentence

A young house-assessor who has condemned his neighbours' homes by the book finds his own family's house starving on a perfect feeding record, and has ten days to prove the city's only feed supplier is selling dust — before he must sign the form that either turns his mother out or leaves his sister asleep in a house that will close on her.

That has opposition (the Wardens' Guild, and the one man in it who is kind to him) and it has moves he has already made: he timed his own wall with the cord, carried a pinch of feed into the yard, used a procedural clause to buy a week, and put his secret into a stranger's hand. No complaint here.

## 2. The world, for that friend

**What's different:** "In Vellmouth a house was grown, not built, and a house ate all its life." Houses grow from a heart-stone — "broad as a barrel-head," live ones "a dark shine to it, like a wet slate" — and must be fed one sack of lime-and-river-silt per room, every seventh day. Starved, a house eats itself, and in a fixed order that makes it readable: "Back rooms first, then the doorways, then the ceilings: a house fed too little fed on itself, from its far end in toward its heart, and always in that order, so the order told you how far it had gone." Old houses hide it: "Old ones hold their shape. Hold it and hold it, and you'd never know. Then they go all at once." Then they *sing* — "the walls groan for some hours before the house closes" — and the walls meet on whoever is inside.

**How it works:** the Wardens' Guild "owned the silt beds on the Vell and sold every sack of feed in the city." You may buy only at your own quarter's yard, on credit, written against your house in the yard's book — and debt is counted in sacks, not coin, because "a family three sacks behind was three weeks from a narrowing door." The Survey Office measures door widths against "The Lost Line, as drawn on the advice of the Wardens' Guild, with a margin for safety," certifies houses lost, and ten days later the clearers take the stone. The family goes to the board-shacks by the dredge cuts — "built and not grown, dead from the day they were put up" — and the plot goes back to the guild to be sold for replanting.

**Who he is:** Iven Sallow, twenty, two years in the grey coat, twenty pence a day — "eight pence more than the docks paid, more than any man on Tanner's Row brought home." He is the office's best pair of hands: "He had been good at it from his first month... took the same doorway three times and get the same figure three times." He has one instrument nobody knows about: sixty knots of twine and a palm flat on plaster, because "a fed house swelled after feeding."

**What he wants:** on the page, to file his own house as not lost and have the figures be honest when he does — "By the tenth day he would file the Sallow house as not lost, in his own small upright hand." By ch4 that has become: get one sack nobody has opened, out of the yard, before it is sold.

**If he fails:** the boards by the cuts, or Wren under a ceiling. Both are shown, not asserted. Also a third thing the book is more interested in: "His seal had got the Dennets out before their house closed on them. His seal had also turned them out."

**Guesses / not on the page:** who E. Barrow is — not on the page, only a name in a buyer's column. Who reported the Sallow house — deliberately sealed. ## 4. Where I skimmed; did anything lecture?

- **ch03, the clearance-file procedure** — "The clearance file was a long book ruled across both pages into columns... a last column for the buyer, headed in a clerk's copperplate, which stayed empty until the guild's slip came back after a sale." ## 7. Where did the biggest thing happen?

On the page, at full length, and it is the best decision in the five chapters: the Orrin house closing occupies most of chapter five in scene, in the dark, with his hands in the gap — "the jamb came on against them, slow as a cart up the Steps and not stopping for anything, and he felt the skin go off the outsides of both his forearms like bark off a stick."

But worth naming: the two acts that define Iven's guilt both happen offstage and are then *retold*. The Dennet certification arrives in past perfect — "A month ago he had walked this house with the brass rule from his coat pocket... and pressed the office seal into the wax" — and the grandmother on the Steps appears only as memory (ch3) and then as legend in someone else's mouth (ch4): "she came out of it sideways. That second one works precisely *because* it is a report — "The Steps had taken it off him... and made a story out of it, and the story had his name in it." So I'd call this a deliberate pattern, not a failure, but the book's moral inciting incident is a recollection.

**The move that repeats — across chapters:** the measurement that is accurate and false. Three figures to a doorway, first and last agreeing, inside the Line. It is run four times with rising cost: the Dennet doorway he measured twice "because it was Hob's house"; his own pantry at "about the width of a fingernail"; Prent's benediction over it, "Clean figures, Sallow"; and then the last page, after a man has died in a house that would have measured just as clean — "He could have taken the same three figures at Abel Orrin's door at dusk, with this same rule, and they would have been just as good, and he would have gone home to his supper." That is a real structural argument, built with the same props each time.

**Across mouths:** nobody in this book says the hard thing to a person — they say it to an object. The foreman tells his horse ("Got 'em out in time, though"). Marra tells the house, the onions, the sacks, the book ("Never behind. Not once"). Bram says "Sallow" to the book. Aurel addresses the stacked sacks. Abel tells the stone. Tansy says it "to nobody." Wren "said it to the road." Iven, at the Orrin gate, speaks "over his shoulder to the street." It is a genuine cultural grammar and it pays off twice — Marra's "Let him go, then," she told the house, and Abel's answer to it. **But it is overused.** By chapter five the oblique-address tag has stopped belonging to characters and become the narrator's accent; I was noticing the device instead of the people. That is my one prose-level complaint and it is the easiest to fix.

## 9. The best beat

> "It's not keeping me," Abel said. "I'm keeping it."

Four pages of rescue have trained me to read this house as a mouth closing on an old man, and the whole magic system as a threat. One sentence turns the direction of force around: the house is not holding him hostage; he is holding the house's death company, and the only loyalty available to him is to die with it rather than go to the boards — which he has already explained in the flattest possible way: "Board walls. Nothing in them... Eighty years I've been in you. I'll not go to the boards." It is also the one thing in five chapters that no measurement, list, form or cord can touch, which is exactly the hole the protagonist is standing in.

Closest second, same chapter, and the better plot beat — Garret Orrin, calm, not sitting down: "Was it on your list?" / "No." / "Then what's your list for?" He does not wait for an answer. Neither do I; there isn't one.

## Verdict: **4.5**

Would recommend it unprompted, with one caveat I'd say out loud. The world is coherent, priced in coin and days, and expressed through habits rather than history; the prose is controlled and nearly always working for the scene; the moral machine — his own seal, the lie he tells his mother to buy a warm kitchen, the fingernail that will be inside the Line right up to the night it isn't — is the actual subject rather than decoration. Chapter five is the real thing. What holds it off a 5 is structural, not sentence-level: at the end of five chapters the opposition has no body, and the hero is a chapter behind me.

### Ranked cost list

1. **The antagonist is a hole where a person should be.** "E. Barrow" is nine lines of ink and a phrase — "Never heard of a Barrow" — and the one face attached to the guild is constructed so that nothing he does on the page can be read as guilt. "Things come in on the barrows" is a brilliant deflection and also the author's alibi for never putting an opponent in a room. Five chapters, and the opposition has done nothing to Iven he can name.
2. **I was ahead of him from chapter two.** When Aurel says "Loose feed goes dry, and dry feed goes light... That's the whole secret," the scam is settled for me. So ch4's jars — the chapter's engine — deliver confirmation rather than discovery: "I mix it black... It goes out of the gate grey." The text knows the gap is there ("A palm was not a column") and argues for it well, but the net result is that Iven's big investigative chapter tells me nothing I hadn't banked.
3. **The clock is granted rather than paid for.** Rule twelve clause three is a lovely character move, and Prent even approves of him for it — "A second measure is exact." But it is the author handing the hero a week at the exact moment the ten-day deadline should start hurting, and he "could not have said what he meant to do with the week." The tenth day never arrives in these pages.
4. **The oblique-address device thickens into a tic.** Roughly two dozen instances across five chapters, in nearly every mouth. It stops individuating anybody.
5. **The narrator explains its own best moves.** "That was what made it a lie." / "and the Shallows was wrong." / chapter-closing thesis sentences that restate the scene just played ("One name had bought the ground under every house in the Shallows that died this season, and three of those houses Iven had certified dead with his own seal").
6. **Iven's convenient blind spot.** A man whose compulsion is finding gaps — "He had once run his eye down the whole wall looking for a gap, because looking for gaps was what his eyes did" — has in two years of palming other people's walls never once palmed his own: "He had never done it here. There had never been a reason." That is the plot's foundation and it is the one thing in the book I didn't believe.
7. **Dropped thread:** eleven houses where seven was the season's worst. Flagged twice in ch1 and never asked again.
8. **Prent and Wren are static.** Both are excellently drawn and neither changes or costs him anything across five chapters; Prent exists to be correct, Wren to be a conscience gauge.

### Three changes that would move it to 5

1. **Give the opposition a body, in one scene, in these pages.** Either put E. Barrow on the page — a receipt with a street on it, a Heights door, a clerk who knows the name — or let Aurel Vane do exactly one thing that cannot be read innocently. The honest place is ch4: instead of Iven leaving Pell's room with "he did not know," let him leave with an address, and let the address be a surprise. Right now the ch3 clearance-file column is the only antagonist in the book, and it is a column.
2. **Make the bought week cost something, on the page, in the scene where he buys it.** Prent slides the form back and "wrote something short in the book she kept for herself." Show the price: she takes two houses off his list and gives them to another junior (so the only lever he had — being the man who measures the Shallows — starts slipping), or she tells him what that private book is for. As written, ch3 is the one chapter where the protagonist gets exactly what he asks for from the institution and pays nothing.
3. **Close the gap between what I know and what he knows, and cut the explain-the-move sentences.** Let Vane's "loose feed goes dry" come *after* Pell's wet-corked jar rather than two chapters before it, so the jars reveal something new — what the grey stuff *is*, not merely that it isn't what Vane said. And strike the three or four sentences where the narrator grades its own work ("That was what made it a lie."; "and the Shallows was wrong."), plus half the "said it to the road / to the sacks / to the book" tags. The scenes are strong enough to be left alone.

## Limitations

- **n = 1 novel**, five chapters, one seed. The three judges agree, but they share a model and a questionnaire, so their agreement is not three independent readers.

- **The blind was partial** (C1, C2). Every judge inferred machine authorship before reading.
- **The showrunner read only ch 1 and ch 5 in full** before its impression.
- **The models changed from run #6**, so the 3.5 → 4.5 cannot be split between the room and the models.
- **Two paths did not arise** (the round-6 re-ask and the round-5 mix), and two serial-memory mechanisms were not due (compression, the every-10 re-read).
