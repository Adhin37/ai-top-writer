# 2026-10-04 — Fan fiction: what the top books do in chapters 1–10

A genre study (protocol: [docs/genre-study.md](../genre-study.md)), stage S4, synthesis. The study's
files are in `research/fanfic/` (gitignored): `books.md`, `progress.md`, the readers' notes in
`notes/<slug>/`. This document carries titles, chapter numbers and paraphrase only; no sentence of
any book is quoted.

## 1. Question and method

**Question.** What makes a reader of serialized fan fiction open chapter *n*+1, and what nearly
stops them, in the books the two platforms rank highest? And where does a reader who knows the
source work (*the fan*) part from one who does not (*the cold reader*)? The room's beta reader is
the cold one.

**The lists.**
- **Royal Road:** *Best Rated*, fan fiction tag. Order taken from a search snippet plus each book's
  page, unverified (the site refuses automated fetches and bars ClaudeBot). Picks 1–3 kept.
- **Webnovel:** the fan-fic power ranking. **Provisional:** titles from search-indexed snapshots of
  unknown date; the ranking moves weekly. Ranks are marked `1?`–`3?` in `books.md`.

**Picks and skips** (`progress.md`). Ten were picked (top five per list). The user saved six books
from their own browser, three per site; four rows were dropped rather than waited for: Pokemon
Trainer Vicky and A Nerubian's Journey (Royal Road 4–5), and two Webnovel rows (a Naruto/MHA
crossover and a Fairy Tail book). No book was skipped for having fewer than ten free chapters.

**What was read.** Chapters 1–10 of each book (Just Deserts: its first ten entries, some of them
parts of a split chapter), about 325k words in all. One Sonnet reader per book, reading one chapter
at a time and writing its notes before opening the next, answering each question as the fan and
as the cold reader. Just Deserts (about 179k words) took four spawns. The synthesis (this
document, Opus) read every `summary.md` and all sixty chapter notes, and the knowledge-base docs
listed in the protocol.

**Limits.** Six books, not ten; the Webnovel ranks are not verified; Just Deserts' ten entries are
twelve times the length of Marvel's, so "chapters 1–10" is not the same quantity of book. Readers
are models answering as a fan, and the fan's half rests on what the model knows of each source.
Two `names.txt` files were partly built by grep (harmless for the leak sweep).

## 2. The books

**Ghost in the City** (Royal Road 1). A modern gamer wakes as a crippled fourteen-year-old girl in
the game's city, lives with her ganger brother, and finds a hidden game interface that turns
pushups, gunplay and hacking into experience points. A gamer self-insert; it sells numbers going
up, a warm sibling bond, canon cameos the narrator recognises, and a small girl surviving a brutal
city by cleverness. Hook curve: strong (ch1), fair, fair–strong, strong (ch4, the first kill and
its cost), fair, weak (ch6, shopping and gym), strong (ch7, the attack on the brother's
workplace), strong (ch8, a near-death fight), fair–strong, fair. The fan pulls harder where cameos
appear; the cold reader holds on the brother.

**Sublight Drive** (Royal Road 2). A man from Earth wakes as a Separatist captain mid-war, seizes
a fleet in chapter 1 when his admiral dies, and uses ship-design snark and film knowledge to bend
the war. A transmigration whose type is told in scattered asides until chapters 5 and 8. It sells
naval tactics against the canonical heroes, a sardonic narrator against military formality, a dry
droid foil, and familiar canon arcs rewritten. Hook curve: strong, fair/strong, fair, fair,
fair/strong, strong (ch6, the blackout), strong (ch7, a captured Padawan), fair, fair, fair. The
fan stays strong throughout; the cold reader falls to weak where the narrator is absent (ch9) and
in the campaign briefing (ch10).

**Just Deserts** (Royal Road 3). A literal-minded orphan with a sand power enters the hero school
to find who took his only friend, and to kill them. An original character in a canon that diverges
at the school's first villain attack, where two named classmates die. It sells a cold planner who
outthinks adults, the canon class in warm comic scenes, and a procedural hunt run inside the
school calendar. Hook curve: strong, fair, strong, fair, strong (ch5, the deaths), fair, strong
(ch7, the vow), strong, fair–strong, fair. Its chapters average about 18k words; its weaknesses
are length, rosters and canon rehearsal.

**Marvel: Emotion Farming System** (Webnovel 1?). A would-be novelist lands in the Marvel world
with a system that pays points for readers' emotions, so he serialises a famous anime film as his
own fiction and courts a canon heroine as his first reader. Transmigration plus system, with an
alternate-universe twist (she is the spider-hero; the canonical hero has no powers). It sells a
low-risk power fantasy and a meter that climbs. Hook curve: fair, weak (ch2, nothing played),
fair, strong (ch4, a cut mid-scene), fair, fair, fair, weak (ch8, the film retold, no Marvel),
strong, strong. The fan of Marvel stalls in the chapters without Marvel; the cold reader meets the
film fresh and does better there.

**Young Sheldon: Twin brother of Georgie** (Webnovel 2?). A teenager reincarnated as a new Cooper
twin, with a gacha wheel (one pull, then a 90-day cooldown) and knowledge of the show's future.
Reincarnation with a system. It sells the family's sitcom warmth, a second genius in the house,
and the chance to save the father. Hook curve: fair, weak, weak–fair, weak (ch2–4, the cheat
unused, school logistics), fair, strong (ch6, the father collapses), strong (ch7), fair,
fair–strong, weak–fair. The fan is ahead of the cold reader in nine chapters of ten.

**DC: Winging a Cursed Life** (Webnovel 3?). A dead man bargains badly with a petty god and is
reborn in Gotham as a reality warper bound to serve a master, punished for every loophole; he
becomes a perfect butler to villains and farms Batman for skills. A reincarnation rules-comedy. It
sells a smug hero out-talking gods and Batman, and a butler persona with a running line. Hook
curve: strong, fair, strong (ch3, the villain's butler), strong, fair, weak (ch6, a recap across
three viewpoints), fair, fair–strong, strong (ch9, the bank), weak–fair. Fan and cold reader are
closest here: the icons are pop culture.

## 3. Findings

### F1. The departure from canon is a premise fact, and the cold reader needs it on page one

**Claim.** Every book that names, in its first chapter and in plain words, *who the protagonist is
relative to canon and how they got there* (reborn, transmigrated, an original character, with a
system or not) is legible to both readers from chapter 1. The one that hides it pays with the cold
reader for half the sample.

**Evidence.** Young Sheldon ch1 states the reincarnation, the date and the cheat in its first
page; the readers rate it the book's one plainly good premise move. Ghost ch1 has the narrator say
in the first screen that she is not who she was and that she knows this city, then the interface
arrives by ¶199. DC ch1 opens on the god's bargain and its terms. Marvel ch1 boots the system with
a mission and a five-day clock. Just Deserts ch1 opens on the power unlocking, with a place stamp.
Against: Sublight ch1–4 signal the transmigration only in asides (films, Earth); the first plain
statement is ch5, the plan stated outright only ch8, and the reader's summary lists the scattered
signal among the book's lessons to avoid.

**Exceptions.** Sublight still holds the cold reader through ch1 on its voice and a self-contained
battle; hiding the type cost clarity, not the click.

**Fan vs cold.** The fan infers the type from genre conventions; the cold reader cannot.

### F2. The arrival is told in the genre's shorthand, then stated plainly

**Claim.** Three books open the transmigration by naming the stock arrival and skipping it (the
waking-in-a-strange-room line played as a joke; the expected white room and god announced as
absent; the god's speech cut off by the hero). The wink buys speed with the genre reader, and it
works for the cold reader only because a plain sentence of what happened follows at once.

**Evidence.** Ghost ch1 (the trope joke, then the plain statement within the first screen), Marvel
ch1 ¶5–7 (no white room, no god; then the system), DC ch1 (the god cuts the hero short; the curse's
terms follow in dialogue). Against: Young Sheldon ch1 ¶3 opens on a coy narrator paragraph about
where the tale starts, which the reader marks as a throat-clear before the plain statement.

**Exceptions.** Just Deserts and Sublight have no arrival scene at all; they open in the life
already under way.

### F3. A canon name the fan knows still needs its clause; a canon roster is a crowd

**Claim.** The cold reader survives canon density when each canon term or person arrives with one
line in the narrator's voice saying what it is; the chapters where names stack without that line
are where the cold reader skims or drops. The writer of fan fiction knows the source, so canon
names feel glossed already: the rule in `orienting-the-reader.md` §4 is the rule, and this is its
fan-fiction case.

**Evidence.** Works: Ghost ch1–2 glosses gangs, scavengers, shards and slang inline (the reader
says the cold reader loses only the in-jokes); DC ch3 uses icons any reader knows; Young Sheldon
ch1 sketches each family member in a line. Fails: Sublight ch3 (a Senate-faction explainer), ch9
(a block of the seer's coined terms), ch10 (a campaign briefing of a dozen place names): the
reader marks the cold reader nearly lost. Just Deserts ch3 (twenty classmates by costume), ch10
(the roll call of schools and forty finishers). Ghost ch9 names two canon figures only by insult,
which tells the cold reader nothing. Marvel ch5 lists other franchises' powers in the shop.

**What worked instead.** Just Deserts' own summary: give three people a gag and summarise the
rest; Sublight's: one place per decision.

**Fan vs cold.** The fan reads the roster as a pleasure (a ritual); the cold reader as a list.

### F4. A canon event pays twice when the insert's own errand moves inside it

**Claim.** The strongest canon chapters use a known arc as the skeleton and put the insert's
private thread in the middle of it, so the canon beat also advances what only this story wants.
The weakest replay a canon scene with the insert watching, or leave the host world for a whole
chapter.

**Evidence.** Works: Just Deserts matches each canon event to a step of the hunt (ch3 battle
training with the yacht lead, ch5 the villain attack with the deaths, ch8–10 the festival as the
road to an internship near the suspect); Sublight ch4–7 runs the canon kidnapping arc with the
insert's ambush as the new fight in it; Young Sheldon ch7 replays the show's hospital night with
the insert taking the wheel, and it is the book's strongest chapter. Fails: Just Deserts ch3 plays
a canon fight of about 2,000 words through monitors with the insert passive; Young Sheldon ch5
replays an early episode's beats with the insert as a bystander delivering one line; Marvel ch4
and ch8 retell another work's plot with no Marvel in them (ch8 is one of the book's two weak
chapters); Sublight ch9 is a chapter with the insert absent, and the cold reader falls to weak.

**Fan vs cold.** The replay is a rerun to the fan and a roster to the cold reader; the moved
event is new to both.

### F5. A canon mind looking at the insert does what the insert cannot

**Claim.** A scene from a canon character's viewpoint, misreading the insert, shows the insert from
outside (menacing, absurd, enviable) without the insert explaining themselves, and it works cold.
It fails when the new viewpoint retells clues the reader already holds.

**Evidence.** DC ch4 (the captives), ch7 (the crime boss's account of his new butler), ch8 (the
fight in the boss's eyes); Marvel ch9–10 (the unpowered hero sees the insert as dangerous; the
reader calls it the reversal that makes the power fantasy human); Sublight ch6 (the dark-side
assassin senses the insert as a blank); Ghost ch6 (a gang teen finds her frightening; an original
character, the same move). Fails: DC ch6 (Batman's week of searching re-explains ch4's clues) and
ch9 (the cave scene recaps ch8); Sublight ch9 hands a whole chapter to canon minds with the insert
off the page.

**Fan vs cold.** The fan enjoys the canon character's voice; the cold reader enjoys the dramatic
irony, which needs no canon.

### F6. The fan holds the canon future; the cold reader holds only what the page said

**Claim.** Foreknowledge from canon gives the fan dread for free and the cold reader nothing,
unless the page says what is coming before the chapter that spends it. One plain line is enough.
Where nothing is spent, the fan-only irony costs nothing.

**Evidence.** Young Sheldon ch6: the father's collapse; the fan knows the show's death, the cold
reader gets one vague line about knowing his heart and cannot tell how bad or how soon (fan strong,
cold fair). Ch7 works for both because the insert says plainly he is afraid of the heart attack in
the first lines. Sublight ch1–8: the film knowledge is spent in asides; the plan is stated only in
ch8. Costless cases: Ghost ch3 (a canon gang boss the narrator does not recognise; the fan does)
and Just Deserts ch4 (a canon villain's cameo at the gate): neither is spent yet, and the cold
reader loses only a wink.

### F7. The scene the reader saw, told again to someone who did not

**Claim.** A character briefing another on events the reader witnessed is the most common skim in
the sample after rosters. It is a cross-chapter fault: each chapter reads as handling its own
business.

**Evidence.** Just Deserts ch7 (about 1,100 words retelling the attack to the suspect's father,
adding only one confession), ch10 (the festival schedule told again at dinner after ch9's
briefing); DC ch6 (¶119–157) and ch9 (the cave, ¶3–72); Marvel opens ch2–5, 7, 9 and 10 by
repeating the last chapter's end; Young Sheldon ch2 and ch3 re-explain the reincarnation and the
cover story.

**Exceptions.** The retelling bit where the listener's reaction was the event (Just Deserts ch7:
the father's confirmation; Sublight ch8: the report to the general, where the argument over the
prisoner is new).

### F8. A joke button over a live thread lets the hook go

**Claim.** Chapters that close on a warm gag while a plot thread is open have the weakest pull of
their books; the readers say outright to end on the thread. A comic last line works when the joke
*is* the cost.

**Evidence.** Ghost ch6, ch9, ch10 (a gym gag, a satisfied beat, a tickle fight; the strong hook,
the brother's new chrome, sits mid-chapter in ch10); Sublight ch10 (a small politeness joke
after a promotion and an impossible mission); DC ch5, ch6 and ch10 (banter, food praise, the
Sunday-off joke); Young Sheldon ch10 (a crush line then a donation joke); Just Deserts ch6 (the
transcript and the line about not wanting to kill her parents mid-chapter, then train bickering and
a gag). Works: DC ch1 ends on the hero cursing the god as the prize vanishes (the joke is the cost);
Ghost ch7's comic last line follows the brother's goodbye and the reader rates it strong, softened.

### F9. A refrain earns its return when it comes back turned

**Claim.** Readers wait for a recurring gag and enjoy it when it is spaced and returns changed; the
same gag becomes the voice's default and wears by the fourth use.

**Evidence.** Just Deserts ch1 runs a child's apology to his uncle as a running joke and then turns
it into the chapter's knife; ch10 pays off an uncle's old defeat three times at spaced intervals,
which the reader calls a Chekhov gag. DC ch4 and ch7 deliver the butler's Victorian insult as a
payoff the reader waits for; by ch10 the butler line is the default voice in every scene and the
reader says the gag wears and the plot goal drops.

### F10. A gift given in chapter 1 is used on the page by chapter 2 or 3

**Claim.** The readers punish a dormant cheat harder than any other early fault: a reward given and
not used is a promise the next chapters break.

**Evidence.** Young Sheldon: the wheel's gift arrives in ch1 and is shown only as fast reading
until ch9; ch2–4 are clothes, haircuts and school logistics, and the cooldown is never mentioned in
ch5 (cold reader weak in ch2–5; the reader names the dormant cheat its weakest technique). Works:
Ghost uses the interface every chapter from ch1, each use paid in a level; DC's power is used and
punished in ch1 and ch2. Partial: Marvel counts points every chapter but the first played event is
ch5–6; the reader rates the early pull as the system's novelty only.

### F11. The title carries the fandom, in each platform's form

**Claim.** All six titles put the source work in the title line, but the two platforms do it
differently: Webnovel leads with the fandom and a colon; Royal Road ends with the fandom, and often
the type, in parentheses or a subtitle.

**Evidence.** Webnovel: Marvel: …, Young Sheldon: …, DC: …. Royal Road: … (Star Wars); … (MHA, OC);
…: Cyberpunk Gamer SI.

### F12–F20. Confirmations

Each is already in a knowledge base; the books confirm it, and the row asks for no edit.

- **F12. Screens right after the action, menus skimmed** (`toggle-litrpg.md`, *Screens are
  documents*). Ghost ch2, ch8, ch9: one- or two-line popups landing after an action, with a wry
  reaction, are the drip of reward; perk menus read aloud (ch2, ch8, ch10) are skimmed by both
  readers. Marvel ch4, 7, 9: a notification naming the feeling and the number is a joke and a score
  in one line; the shop tiers (ch5) are skimmed. DC ch1–2: each rule arrives as a one-line patch
  stamped in by pain.
- **F13. The rule at the moment it bites; the block where nobody wants anything**
  (`orienting-the-reader.md` §1, §3). Bit: DC ch1 (curse terms in the god's dialogue), Ghost ch2 ¶223
  and ch4 ¶503 (a mechanic explained at the moment of use), Just Deserts ch7 (a casualty argument in
  dialogue with a friend's place at school at stake), Ghost ch4 (a heist briefing). Skimmed: Just
  Deserts ch2 (a 650-word analysis of why the exam exists) and ch8 (a 1,400-word registry lecture),
  Sublight ch9 (ten paragraphs of a general's past), Young Sheldon ch6 (1,200 words of summary).
- **F14. The event is played** (`scene-and-summary.md`, `delivery-test.md`). DC ch2, ch3, ch7 summarise
  their best set-pieces; Young Sheldon ch5 (the FBI visit), ch8 (the principal), ch10 (the doctor) report
  the chapter's event; Marvel ch2 tells the upload in numbers. Each is marked in the readers' weakest
  three. DC ch3's one-week skip bit, on a single funny image: summary doing its job.
- **F15. The cool chapter still has a want** (`arcs-and-chapters.md`). The weakest chapter of most books
  is a lull with no want: Ghost ch6, DC ch6, Young Sheldon ch2–4, Marvel ch2 and ch8. Just Deserts ch6 is
  a friends' day out that holds at fair on one line of want mid-chapter.
- **F16. Nobody lowered for the insert's win** (`never-stupid.md`; the out-of-character budget in
  `toggle-fanfic.md`). DC ch8–9: Batman cut up by forks, a canon brute accepting a civilian's bargain;
  the reader says every authority falling for the same trick drains the menace. Ghost ch9: a canon fixer
  used as a cheap villain. Against: Sublight ch5 gives a canon crime lord a sympathetic father's interior
  with its reasons on the page, and both readers accept the stretch.
- **F17. Recap folded into one clause** (`openings-and-endings.md`). Marvel's repeated-ending openings
  (seven of ten chapters), Young Sheldon ch2's re-explanation, Ghost ch9's restless recap start: each is
  marked as the first skim of its chapter.
- **F18. The beta reader stays cold** (principle 2; `beta-reader/prompt.md`). Where the two readers
  parted, the fan pulled harder in almost every chapter; the exceptions (Marvel ch8, Just Deserts ch1)
  ran on non-canon material. A chapter that works cold worked for the fan; the reverse failed often. The
  cold read is the binding test, and it is the one the room already runs.
- **F19. One head per scene** (`toggle-pov-switch.md`). Young Sheldon ch1 and ch3, Marvel ch3 and ch4,
  DC ch4 slip into a second head without a break; each reader marks it.
- **F20. The stage direction** (`ai-default-habits.md`). DC ch2–4 fill beats with written pauses and
  one-word paragraphs; Just Deserts uses dash chains and a speech-tag tic throughout. Both are marked as
  prose the reader tolerates for the dialogue.

## 4. The platforms

| | Royal Road picks | Webnovel picks |
|---|---|---|
| chapter length (median of ten) | Ghost ~5.0k, Sublight ~4.2k, Just Deserts ~17.8k words | Marvel ~1.4k, Young Sheldon ~2.0k, DC ~1.95k words |
| fandoms | deep-lore: a game, the Clone Wars, a shōnen series | pop-common: Marvel, DC, a sitcom |
| system or cheat | one of three (Ghost) | all three (points meter, gacha wheel, god's rules) |
| fan vs cold gap | wide: Sublight ch9–10 and Just Deserts' rosters lose the cold reader | narrow: icons any reader knows; DC nearly equal throughout |
| the weak stretch | single chapters (Ghost ch6) | runs of chapters (Young Sheldon ch2–4) |
| author's notes | rare | in-chapter pleas for power stones, Patreon or paywall plugs, a donation joke: the readers mark them puncturing the hook (Young Sheldon ch10, Marvel ch3–4) |
| title form | fandom last, in parentheses or a subtitle | fandom first, with a colon |

Webnovel's picks are short, daily-sized chapters on pop-culture fandoms with a game layer, and they
recap at the top of chapters (Marvel), which suits a reader returning daily and costs a reader who
binges. Royal Road's picks are longer, darker (a revenge hunt, a war) and assume more of the source;
their weak points are rosters, lore blocks and length, not dormant premises. Prose polish does not
separate the lists: all six carry typos, tense slips and head-hopping, and the readers forgave them
for voice (Sublight, DC), the game loop (Ghost) or canon warmth (Young Sheldon).

## 6. Not proposed

- **Polish does not rank these books.** All six carry typos, tense slips and head-hopping, and rank
  anyway. The room polishes regardless; nothing here argues for less, and "readers forgive bad
  prose" is no instruction to a role.
- **Author's notes and platform pleas** (Webnovel). They puncture hooks (Young Sheldon ch10,
  Marvel ch3–4), but the room writes no author's notes.
- **The intensifier stack** (Young Sheldon throughout; DC ch1). Two books, but a new row in the line
  editor's catalog would be appended, not replace one; the existing *stage direction* and *stock
  phrases* rows already catch the worst of it.
- **The dry foil who turns exposition into banter** (Sublight's droid). One book, and
  `orienting-the-reader.md` §2 already holds the move (someone with a reason to ask).
- **Persona and catchphrase as a comic engine** (DC). One book for the engine itself; its wear is
  in F9.
- **Chapter length.** The Webnovel picks run 1.4–2.7k words and the Royal Road picks 3–24k. Length
  is a platform setting in `novel.md`, not a finding about craft, and Just Deserts' 18k-word
  chapters are its readers' main complaint, not a model.
- **Content that the book does not react to** (DC ch2's drugging and grooming plans; Young Sheldon
  ch10's adult-mind crush on a married woman; Just Deserts ch9's surveillance of a woman
  undressing). The readers flag discomfort, but no reader dropped on it in the notes, and content
  limits are `novel.md`'s `content` keys and the bias docs' business.
- **Divergence count.** `toggle-fanfic.md` asks for one divergence point. Marvel (an alternate
  universe plus a crossover bloodline) and Sublight (several canon changes, including a crossover
  backstory) carry more, and the readers do not mark it as a fault, only the cold reader's load in
  the explaining blocks (F3). Two books cannot show whether a second unrelated change costs a
  reader; the rule stays.
- **A fan beta reader.** The study's most useful split would argue for a second, fan reader. F18
  shows the cold read is the binding one, and a second reader is a role, not a doc edit.
