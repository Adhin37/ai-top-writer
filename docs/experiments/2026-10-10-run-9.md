## 1. The story in one sentence

A fifteen-year-old lamplighter in an occupied river town is the only person alive whose fire is precise enough to burn one rice terrace exactly as the occupiers want it burned — so she spends twelve chapters trying to make the burning survivable for her mother's family on that hill, while the officer who picked her quietly collects a hostage out of everyone Kesa has ever helped.

Would follow the serial and would recommend it to the right person without being asked — which is the only thing holding it off a 5: I would caveat the recommendation. ### Ranked cost list, biggest first

1. **The central event is still pending at the end of chapter 12, and the book now says so out loud.** "Sixteen markets... Eighty days and a few more, and then noon on the ninth, at the dry, with her hand at the fire" (ch12). Twelve chapters of pressure resolve into a timer. The wall collapse in ch10 is a strong substitute climax, but it is a side plot's climax.
2. **The narration explains signals the reader has already read.** The "second time, the frightened one" is labelled in four separate chapters; the "lit late" blessing is glossed three times; the heel bet and the fallen wall get full recap paragraphs (ch09 line 83, ch12 line 25). Each instance costs a little trust.

It works because the captain's two hundred pages of manners collapse into one physical gesture on two pieces of rubbish — a slip of paper and a fish wrapper — and both of them are things the reader watched being made, eleven chapters and one chapter ago respectively. *"It will keep, will it not"* has been said twice before as a courtesy; here it becomes the hand on Kesa's throat. No threat is stated. The thumb does it.

Runner-up, and the beat I will actually remember: Kuro on step twenty-nine with his foot in the air, and *"'Thirty,' he said, as if it had been his idea."* Four years of a man who can only say *"Oil's there"* and *"Go on"*, explained in nine lines — and it is also where Kesa's exactness, and her habit of answering precisely what was asked and then stopping, both come from: *"And she saw where she had got it from... She had always thought it was hers."*

## Verdict: **4.5**

I would follow this serial and I would recommend it unprompted, with one caveat I would have to say out loud: nothing has burned yet, and chapter twelve tells me how long I have to wait.

### Ranked cost list (biggest first)

1. **The clock keeps being reset.** The burning is proposed on page one of ch01 and is still unstarted at the end of ch12. Three deferrals in a row: *"Three months off, that puts it"* (ch03), then a practice burn, then rain, then a *second* practice burn that does the same job as the first, and finally ch12 prices the wait at *"Sixteen markets... Seventeen"* — eighty-odd days. The chapter ends counting to ten days for a **yes**, not for a fire. Twelve chapters of approach is a lot of approach.
2. **Recap paragraphs in the back half.** ch09–ch12 keep stopping to re-explain the heel, the bet, the stones, the fold, the joins, in full sentences, to a reader who has been there. It flattens the prose exactly where the plot tightens.
3. **The captain is never wrong, and never surprised.** She reads the gap from the boat, has already walked the terraces in the dark, already knows whose brother Gao is, already has the hawk slip cut and in her sleeve, catches the fold, and treats the one thing Kesa wins — the slope — with *"Very well."* By ch11 Kesa's only victory is one the captain then improves on. Thrilling to ch05; after that it quietly removes the possibility of hope, which costs tension rather than adding it.
4. **The tics harden into formula.** Having read it once I counted: the gloves are smoothed/pressed 15 times across 9 chapters; the captain's *"will it not / is it not"* tag lands 10 times; the grandfather/two-fingers combination 13 times; the said-twice-and-unfinished-sentence family 21. In ch11–12 I was predicting the gesture before the line arrived.
5. **Yan is the thinnest of the majors.** For six chapters she is a thumb in a wall plus *"Garrison's girl"*; her ch10 scene is superb but it is the first time she has an inside.
6. **The largest consequence is only ever reported.** Dao's valley is the engine of the whole book and no one in the present has seen it.

### Three changes that would move it up one point

1. **Collapse the two strip burns into one, and shorten the harvest clock on the page.** ch06 (fire gets away) and ch09 (fire stops on her wet line) teach the reader the same rule twice; fold the wet-slope conspiracy and the uncontrolled burn into a single noon, so the ch07 drill-and-rain lull is unnecessary. Then change the hen woman's ch12 line from *"Last," she said. "Sixteen markets... Seventeen."* to four or five markets. Same scene, same gesture of weighing the head on her palm, and the book ends on a deadline I can feel instead of a season I have to wait out.
2. **Let Kesa keep one thing the captain does not get.** ch11 already stages this and then gives it away: *"The plan had had a hole in it, and the hole was the head channel, and, as far as Kesa could tell, the captain had not known. Kesa had stood at this table and filled it."* Make her keep that hole — have her get the men onto the channel on some other pretext — so there is one live card in her hand going into the burning. As written, every concealment is either seen (the gap, the slope, the fold) or filed for later (the heel, the hill light), and a reader needs one that held.
3. **Cut the back-half recaps to a clause.** Specifically: the paragraph beginning *"On market day somebody from the hill had pressed the taken boy's two stones flat in the night"* (ch09) and the paragraph beginning *"It was standing. Twenty paces of her grandfather's small stones"* (ch12) — both are twenty-odd lines of ground I had already walked. One sentence each. The book's own method is *"He left the rest for her"*; these paragraphs are the author refusing to leave the rest for me.
