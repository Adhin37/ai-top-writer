# The cadence test

Two speakers can sit far apart in `bible/cast/_voices.md` and still sound like one person, because
the drafter's rhythm leaks into everybody. Take the longest turn of each speaker in the scene and
compare their **shape**: how many clauses, where the sentence turns, how it lands. If they match,
re-shape one of them to the cadence their row gives (floods then stops, builds in even clauses,
starts flat and sharpens, never finishes, answers before the question ends).

## Patterns

### Two rows, one rhythm

> **Kell:** "No. It isn't negligence. It's just the regulation."
> **Tovi's brother, nine:** "It's day six. Same as yesterday was day five."

Both turns are a short denial or fact, then a flat sentence landing on a bare assertion. The man and
the child have different rows and one voice. The child's row says he argues by repetition and never
finishes:

> **Tovi's brother:** "It's day six. You said thirty. You said—"

### The narrator's cadence in a speaker's mouth

The chapter's narration builds in balanced pairs (*not this, but that*), and by the third scene
every speaker is doing it too. Find each speaker's own build in their row, and give it back to them
in their longest turn first. That is usually the one the reader hears.

### Too uniform a gear

Every speaker in the scene answers in about twelve words. Lint's `stats` line does not show it; the
page does, as paragraphs of the same length down the screen. Move one speaker to their row's usual
turn length, short or long.

## When to leave it

Two characters who share a cadence because they share a life (twins, a couple of forty years, a
copy and its original) may sound alike on purpose. Then the scene should let someone notice it.
