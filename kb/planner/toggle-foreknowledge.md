# A protagonist who knows what comes next

Foreknowledge (a reborn life, a known story, a prophecy, a time loop) fails two ways: as an
**oracle**, accurate, unlimited and free, so every scene is a formality; or as a **handicap**,
unreliable from the start, so the reader was promised an advantage and shown a disability. The
working shape is a resource: it has a **grain** (impressions, events, dates, exact words), a cost
each time it is spent, and a decay the protagonist causes by changing things. It works first, then
costs, then frays, then betrays.

## Examples

### The order

```
ch 2    works     Tovi knows the Deck Four main fails on the third shift, and is there with the clamp
ch 6    costs     she saves the deck again, and Kell starts asking how she always knows
ch 14   frays     her early warnings changed the maintenance rota; the failures she remembers stop coming on time
ch 22   betrays   she prepares for the fire she remembers on Deck Six; it comes on Deck Two, where she
                  moved the crew that would have stopped it
```

### The grain

```
grain       events: she remembers what happened and roughly when, never exact words or dates
inventory   the Deck Four main (ch 2) · the council audit (ch 15) · the Deck Six fire (ch 22)
spent       ch 2: the main · ch 6: the second leak
```

An item not on the inventory is something she does not know. Remembering that a skill exists is
not being able to do it; skills are learned at the normal speed.

### Win before fail

The first several uses must work, and visibly pay off, before the first one fails. The reader
needs to believe in the advantage before they can fear losing it.

### The observer

Someone notices that she always knows. That is a thread: they will want to know how, and the
protagonist must decide what to tell them.

## When to break it

A protagonist who is genre-savvy (knows how stories like this usually go) does not have
foreknowledge. That is a voice, not this module.
