# A game-like system layer

A system layer is a legitimate structure, and the fastest way to replace a story with a spreadsheet.
Before any stat, answer in `bible/power-system.md`: who made it, who sees it, whether it can be wrong
or gamed, **who benefits from people believing it**, and **what it does not measure**. That last is
where character lives. A system that is true, universal and impartial gives you nothing to write
about. Every number has a physical referent the reader learns early, and levels are the novel's one
power ladder, not a second one.

## Examples

### A system with an interest

**A scoreboard:**

> Everyone on the Stack has a visible Grade from 1 to 10, assigned fairly by the Stack's systems.

**A system with an interest:**

> The council's meters assign a Grade every shift. Everyone can see their own and nobody else's,
> except supervisors, who see their crew's. It measures air drawn and fault reports logged. It does
> not measure faults prevented, which is why the best fitters have the worst grades.

The last sentence is a plot, and it is the protagonist's.

### Numbers that mean something

```
Pressure rating 10   a fitter can hold a burst main shut by hand for a minute
Pressure rating 20   for ten minutes, or a Deck Nine main for one
```

If the reader cannot say what 14 lets someone do that 12 did not, the numbers are decoration.

### Screens are documents

A status screen is on the page as an object, in the meta channel (`[…]`, per the format spec), and
never twice in a row. Show the change, not the whole sheet:

```
[ Grade: 6 → 4 · reason logged: fault caused (Deck Four main) ]
```

## When to break it

A story where the system is only ever seen from outside (a world where everyone else has levels and
the protagonist cannot see any) can leave the numbers off the page entirely. Then the system is a
power and a prejudice, not a display.
