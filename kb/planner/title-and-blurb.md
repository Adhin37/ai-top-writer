# Title and blurb

A reader in a platform's grid gives a title about two seconds and a blurb about fifteen. A title
does three jobs: **shelf** (what kind of book: one word with genre freight), **promise** (the
specific pleasure, not the category) and **hook** (why this one and not its neighbours: a
contradiction, a refusal, a destination stated too boldly). Offer three candidates built by
different strategies, so the user chooses between real alternatives rather than three rewordings.
Then write the blurb against the chosen title, so the two promise the same book.

## Examples

The world is invented: the sealed mining habitat of the premise example, where air is billed by job
grade.

### Five strategies, then keep three

| strategy | shape | candidate |
|---|---|---|
| role + fracture | the protagonist's job, broken by one word | *The Fitter Who Breathed on Credit* |
| first-person claim | an intent, a refusal, a flat fact | *I Have Thirty Days of Air* |
| the mechanism | the central rule as a noun phrase | *The Air Ledger* |
| understated flat | one or two plain words | *Down-Well* |
| the destination | where the reader is promised they will get | *The Girl Who Came Back Up* |

No two share a strategy or a first word. Screen each:
- cut it at 30 characters: does the half that survives still say something?
- could a friend spell it into a search box after hearing it once?
- does it promise the book `opening.promise` promises? A title that promises another book converts
  a reader and loses them at chapter 2. This screen is fatal; the others are trade-offs to report;
- is it still true at chapter 100?
- delete *Chronicles, Saga, Legacy, Rise of, Eternal*: if nothing was lost, they were filler.

Present the three that survive best, each with what it promises and what it costs ("*Down-Well*:
dry and confident; says nothing about air until chapter 1 does").

### A blurb

**A summary:**

> Tovi Brand is a pipe-fitter on the Ansel Stack who loses her job grade. She must find work before
> her thirty days of credit run out, and discovers a conspiracy among the deep crews.

**A blurb**, 72 words:

> On the Ansel Stack there is no outside, and every breath is billed. Your job grade decides how
> much of it is paid for.
>
> Tovi Brand reported a leaking air main. For that, her supervisor struck her grade, and she has
> thirty days of air on credit. On day thirty-one, she goes down-well.
>
> The only crews hiring the ungraded are the deep crews. Nobody on the Stack has ever met anyone
> who came back from one.

Three moves: the world and its rule in the first sentence; the protagonist and the problem, with a
clock; an ending on a threat or a question. A blurb never ends on a summary, and it promises nothing
the first five chapters do not start to pay.

## When to break it

Fan fiction puts the source work in the title line (*Source Work: The Title*), because readers
browse fan fiction by fandom. And when the user arrives with a title they love, screen it, report
what the screen found once, and use theirs.
