# Battles

A battle is not a long fight: nobody knows where everybody is, including the people in command.
Pick one thing the reader follows (one person's hundred yards, a commander's choices from inside
bad information, or one unit's job) and hold it. At every moment the reader can answer: what is
this person trying to do, what would losing look like for them, and how would they know?
Information is the resource battles are fought with.

## Examples

**The map with arrows, which nobody inside a battle sees:**

> The eastern pump crews, three hundred strong, advanced along Deck Six while the security
> detachment flanked through the vent shafts, pinning the strikers against the bulkhead.

**One person, one job, bad information:**

> Tovi's job was the valve on Six-East: hold it open so the strikers on Seven could breathe. She
> could hear the fighting two decks up as a change in the vents, a pitch she'd never heard before.
> A runner came down the ladder, white-faced. "They've got Seven," he said. Then: "I think."

The reader knows her objective, what losing means (Seven stops breathing), and how news reaches her:
late, partial, from someone who might be wrong. The outcome reaches the reader the way it reaches
her.

**Scale by sense, not by number:** an order that takes an hour to reach the far end and comes back
wrong; the noise that drowns every shout; what people are eating; where the dead are being put; the
wing you have only heard about.

**Armies lose to supply, sickness and movement** more often than to the enemy. A campaign is
won or lost at the water, the food and the roads, and one line about what the crews are short of
tells the reader more than a casualty count.

## When to break it

A chapter told from above (a commander with a map, maps that are wrong) can work once, if the
commander is a viewpoint character whose orders are about to fail. The reader watches the arrows
and knows the arrows are lying.
