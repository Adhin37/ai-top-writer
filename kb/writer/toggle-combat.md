# Fights

A fight is a scene: someone wants something, is opposed, and leaves changed. Before the first blow,
know what can be lost besides a life (the reader assumes the protagonist lives), how it ends and at
what price, the moment it becomes a different fight, three features of the ground that will be
used, and what each side is better at. On the page, the reader must always know where the bodies
are. Compress the routine exchanges and spend words on the one that decides it.

## Examples

**Exchange by exchange, and the reader is lost by the third line:**

> He struck high and she parried, then he swept low and she jumped, then she thrust and he
> sidestepped, and she spun and struck again, and he blocked with his forearm.

**Compressed, then expanded on the beat that matters:**

> Three passes. He was faster than she had assumed, and he wanted the ladder behind her more than
> he wanted her.
> She gave him the ladder. When he went for it she put her shoulder into the rail, and the rail
> was the only thing holding the scaffold to the wall.

The reader knows the stakes (the ladder), the terrain (rail, scaffold, wall) and the turn (she gives
him what he wants, and it costs him).

**Intention, not technique:** not "he used the Rising Crane" but "he went for the elbow, because
the elbow would end it and he wanted it ended".

**Injuries persist.** A knee to the ribs in the first exchange changes what she can do in the last,
and the morning after.

## When to break it

A fight seen by someone who cannot follow it (a child, a bystander, the wounded) may be a blur on
purpose: then the confusion is the point, and the reader learns the outcome when they do.
