# Writing in someone else's world

Two readers open every chapter: the fan, who knows the source better than you, and a reader who
has never met it. Write so the second can follow and the first is never bored: the fan's pleasure
is recognition, and the other reader's is the story. You know the source, so you cannot feel which
canon names are strange on the page; read your draft as someone who has not seen a minute of it.

## Examples

The source work in these examples is invented: a show called *Deck Nine*, set on a sealed mining
habitat. Borrow the moves, never the nouns.

### The arrival

**The stock arrival, played in full:**

> Mara floated in a white void. A voice spoke from everywhere. "Mara Quill, you have died before
> your time, and I offer you a second life…"

**The genre's shorthand, then the plain fact:**

> No white void, no voice, no apology from anyone. One moment Mara was under a car in a garage in
> Leeds; the next she was sixteen, in a cadet's bunk on Deck Four of the Ansel Stack, in a body
> that was not hers, eight months before the first episode of a show she had watched three times.

A genre reader knows the waking scene by heart; naming it and skipping it buys speed. The sentence
after it is what the other reader needs: who she was, who she is now, where, and when.

### A canon name

**Known to you, so it feels glossed:**

> She saw the deck chief at the Four-Six junction, which meant the audit was already running.

**The same line, with its clause:**

> She saw the deck chief at the Four-Six junction: Hollis, who ran Deck Four by rota and favour
> and had never once thanked anyone aloud. If he was down here at shift change, the council's
> audit had already started.

The rule is [orienting-the-reader.md](orienting-the-reader.md) §4: a name arrives with what it is.
In fan fiction the trap is that every canon name feels explained already. A whole canon roster on
stage is a crowd: give two or three of them a line and an action, and let the rest be "the cadet
class", "the second shift".

### A canon mind looking at the protagonist

**The protagonist explaining herself:**

> Mara knew the others thought her strange. She had known the inspector would be on Deck Six, and
> it was hard to hide that kind of thing.

**A canon character misreading her, after a scene break:**

> * * *
>
> The new cadet had cleared the Deck Six roster an hour before the fire. Hollis put the roster in
> his drawer, on top of the one from the Deck Four leak, which she had also cleared an hour early.
> Somebody was paying her. He would find out who.

From outside, she is a threat or a puzzle, and the reader enjoys knowing what he gets wrong. A
switch like this is governed by `novel.md`'s viewpoint keys
([toggle-pov-switch.md](toggle-pov-switch.md)). Give the new head something the reader has not
seen: his conclusion, not a retelling of the leak.

## When to break it

An alternate universe written for the fandom alone (the same cast in a coffee shop) can lean on
the fan: its readers chose it by the characters' names. Even then, the protagonist's situation is
said plainly in chapter 1.
