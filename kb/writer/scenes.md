# Scenes

A scene is not a location or a conversation. It is a stretch in which someone **wants** something,
is **opposed** by a person, rule or scarcity with its own reason, and **leaves changed**. The best
exits are "yes, but" (got it, and it made a problem) and "no, and" (failed, and it got worse). A
scene the protagonist simply wins is one the reader skims.

## Examples

### The exit is a change, not an outcome

**Plain yes, so the reader has nothing to carry out of the room:**

> Tovi asked the stores clerk for the right-gauge clamp. He found one in the back and signed it out
> to her.

**Yes, but, so she has the clamp and a new problem:**

> The stores clerk found the right-gauge clamp in the back and pushed the chit across. "Sign for
> it." The chit had a line for the supervisor's mark, and Kell's was already on it, dated this
> morning, an hour before the leak was logged.

### Three people in one scene

At three speakers a scene changes category. Give each person one job: one **driver** who wants
what the scene is about, one **blocker** who wants something incompatible, and anyone else as
**pressure**, with business of their own that will not wait. Decide who is silent, and keep them
in the room with one small action a page.

> Kell had the fault log open on the desk and his rag in his other hand. Tovi stood across from
> him. Ossie hovered by the door with her kit, because nobody had told him he could put it down.
>
> "You logged it at six," Kell said. "The alarm tripped at five-forty."
> "The alarm tripped because—"
> "Shift's changing," the clerk said from the counter, not looking up. "I need the chits signed
> before the bell or nobody's billed right." He held out the pen to whoever would take it.

The driver is Kell, and the blocker is Tovi. The clerk is pressure: he wants the chits signed,
and his clock pushes the confrontation along. Ossie, silent, is placed once and stays in the
reader's eye.

### Somebody has to give ground

Before a bargaining scene, know four things for each side: what they **ask** out loud, what they
would really **settle** for, what they hold that the other **values**, and what happens to them if
they **walk away**. Whoever can survive no deal longer has the leverage, and the weaker side wins
only by changing what walking away costs the other.

> "Deep crew pays in air," the recruiter said. "Ninety days of it, up front."
> "I've got thirty days of credit. I don't need ninety."
> "You need it for your brother."
> She did. He knew she did; that was the job. "Then put his name on the chit, not mine. If I don't
> come back, he keeps the air."
> He looked at her a long moment. Nobody had asked for that before. It cost him nothing, and it
> meant she'd sign today.

### The follow-through

After a hard scene, a short passage of reaction, dilemma and decision. It ends on the decision,
which is the next scene's want. Skipped, the chapter is a highlight reel; overrun, the story stops.

> She didn't go back to the bunk. She sat on the stairs to Deck Five with the recruitment slip on
> her knee until the lights dimmed for night shift, and then she folded it in four and put it in
> her boot.

## When to break it

A scene can be all pressure and no winner: a funeral, a waiting room, the minutes before a verdict.
Then the change is in the reader, who leaves knowing what everyone is afraid of.
