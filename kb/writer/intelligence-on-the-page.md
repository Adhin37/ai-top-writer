# Intelligence on the page

A character's intelligence shows in **prediction**: they notice the detail others missed and act a
beat early, or they say what will happen and a page later it does. Every conclusion they reach
traces to something on the page they were given, saw or worked out. Being clever is not a source,
and every plan that works costs something it did not have to. Reading people is a different gift
from reasoning, and a sharp mind that misreads the powerful is a character, not a mistake.

## Examples

### Told and shown

**Told:**

> Tovi was the best fitter on Deck Four, and she understood at once what Kell had done.

**Shown: she predicts, and the page proves her:**

> "He'll say I caused it," Tovi said. "He'll say the clamp was mine."
> Ossie laughed. "It wasn't your clamp."
> At the shift office Kell had the clamp on his desk, tagged, with her number on the tag.

### The trace test

Before a character concludes something, find the sentence it came from.

> She looked at the chit again. Kell's mark, dated this morning. The leak was logged at six. He had
> signed for the right clamp an hour before anyone knew a clamp was needed.

The reader saw the chit in the last scene. The deduction is fair because the reader could have made
it. If no such sentence exists, plant it earlier or cut the deduction.

### A plan shown in part

Show the objective and the first steps, and withhold one element visibly. The withheld piece must
have been bought or noticed earlier, doing another job.

> "Two things," Tovi said. "I get the stores ledger off the clerk before shift change, and you
> keep Kell at the pump hall for ten minutes."
> "And the third thing?"
> "There isn't a third thing."
> There was. It was in her boot, and it had Kell's mark on it.

Then let the plan's assumption fail, and the goal be won differently and at more cost. A plan that
works exactly as stated is one the reader didn't need to see.

### Clever, and wrong about people

> Kell smiled at her across the desk, and Tovi took it for what it looked like: a man who knew he'd
> lost and was being decent about it. She thanked him. On the way out the clerk caught her eye and
> very slightly shook his head.

Her reasoning about the leak was right; her reading of Kell is wrong, and someone with better eyes
for people saw it. The gap between the two gifts is where the story is.

### Thinking at length, broken up

A long chain of reasoning is a lecture in the protagonist's head. Break it across a line of speech,
an action and one short interior beat, and let the last step happen on the page, out loud, in front
of someone who can object.

## When to break it

A character who is out of their depth (exhausted, grieving, outside their trade) may reason badly,
and should. The page shows why, so the reader sees the cause, not an author lowering their
protagonist to make the plot work.
