# Orienting the reader

Your reader has read nothing but your pages. You know the world; they do not, and you cannot feel
the difference from the inside. So by the end of chapter 1 a reader must be able to say, in plain
words: **what is different about this world and how it works**, **who this person is and what
they want right now**, and **what happens to them if it goes wrong**. Anything they would have to
guess, you have not written yet.

The skill is not withholding. It is **putting the explanation where the reader already wants it**
— at the moment the rule bites someone they have just met.

## Examples

The world in these examples is invented. Borrow the moves, never the nouns.

### 1. The rule, stated at the moment it bites

**Dropped in — the reader is lost:**

> Ysolde had four hours until the Long Ebb, and the Calder boy was still on the tally. "Third year,"
> her uncle said, as if that settled it.

**Oriented — same scene, two plain sentences added where they hurt:**

> Ysolde had four hours until the Long Ebb. Once a year the sea at Saltmere drew back past the
> harbour bar and gave back everyone it had drowned since the last one — walking, cold, not quite
> who they had been — and by dawn somebody had to row each of them out past the bar again, or
> they stayed. The Calder boy had been on her tally for three years running. His mother had never
> once come down to the water to help.

The added sentences state the rule in words a reader could repeat to a friend, and they arrive
exactly when a named person is about to pay for it. Four hours, three years, one mother: the rule
is now a problem somebody has.

### 2. Someone who needs to know asks

**Wooden — two people telling each other what both already know:**

> "As you know, your air allowance is set by your job grade," the clerk said.

**Working — the question comes from someone who genuinely does not know, and the answer costs the
person giving it:**

> "Why does it matter what grade they give you?" her brother asked.
> "Because grade is air." Tovi kept her eyes on the meter. "Every shift the Stack bills you for what
> you breathe, and your grade decides how much it pays for. No grade, they give you thirty days on
> credit." She did not say what happened on day thirty-one. He was nine.

A child, a new hire, a foreigner, an official taking a statement — anyone with a real reason not to
know. The answer is short, in the speaker's voice, and it leaves out the part that hurts most, which
is the part the reader now wants.

### 3. The rules as somebody's problem

**A briefing — plain, well placed, and skimmed:**

> "Meter's the meter," Dask said, and went back to his tea.
>
> Everyone on the Stack knew the arithmetic. The air came up from the processors on Deck Nine, it
> was counted by the litre, and it was billed every shift to whoever breathed it. The Gradehall set
> each worker's grade once a year, and the grade decided how much air the Stack paid for. Meters
> were sealed by the Gradehall's own engineers, and a fitter who broke a seal lost her grade. There was
> no appeal. Nobody argued with a meter.
>
> "It's billing him for two," Tovi said.

**The same rules, as what she is weighing — read:**

> "Meter's the meter," Dask said, and went back to his tea.
>
> "It's billing him for two." Tovi tapped the glass. Her brother was nine and slept curled like a
> cat; he did not breathe for two. Every litre that came up from Deck Nine was billed to whoever
> breathed it, and her grade paid for his until he had one of his own, so the meter was spending
> her. She could have the seal off in a minute with the kit on her belt. But the seal was the
> Gradehall's, and a fitter who broke one lost her grade, and then she would be paying for both of them
> on a meter she did not trust.
>
> She left the seal on and started writing the readings down.

The first paragraph comes right after the question, in one plain block, and a reader in a hurry
still slides past it. Nobody is in it and nobody wants anything in it. It stops the argument at its
hottest line, and its last sentence says what Dask just said. What the reader skips is the rules.

In the second, every rule is something Tovi is weighing: what the meter takes from her, and what
opening it would cost. Every sentence, not only the first. A paragraph that opens on her question
and then hands the rules to the narrator is the first version with a hook in front of it. The reader reads each one because she is deciding something, and the scene
moves again on her decision. Dask's line keeps its job, so the paragraph does not say it twice. The
yearly grading and the missing appeal wait for the scene where somebody appeals.

Chapter 1 usually needs one passage like this, where the premise is said plainly. That is fine.
What makes a passage a lecture is not how plain or how long it is. It is that nobody in it wants
anything.

### 4. A name arrives with what it is

**Unglossed — three names the reader has to hold without knowing why:**

> She passed the Tallyhouse and the Reeve's steps and turned down Quorrel Row.

**Glossed — one name the reader needs, with what it does:**

> She passed the Tallyhouse, where the harbour kept its list of the drowned, and turned down toward
> the water.

A name the reader will need again gets a clause saying what it is the first time. A name they will
not need yet becomes a common noun ("the harbour office", "the old warden"). Every coined word is a
small bill the reader pays; in chapter 1 keep the bill short.

## Check your draft as a stranger

When the draft is done, answer these as someone who has read **only this chapter** — not the beat
sheet, not the bible:

1. What is different about this world, and how does it work? *(the mechanism, not just its name)*
2. Who is the protagonist, and what do they want by the end of this chapter?
3. What happens to them if it goes wrong?
4. Which names did I use that the page never explains?

If any answer needs something only you know, put it on the page: in the scene if it fits, or in one
passage where someone is weighing it (example 3) if it does not. The beat sheet's `learns` line lists what this chapter owes; every
item on it should be answerable from the page.

## When to break it

A mystery may withhold *the answer* — who did it, what the thing in the cellar is — but the reader
must always know *what the question is*. Being curious is a pleasure; being lost is not, and
readers cannot tell "deliberately unexplained" from "badly written" on page one. After the premise
has landed, later chapters can go lean: the reader has the frame, and every new fact can arrive the
same way, at the moment it bites.
