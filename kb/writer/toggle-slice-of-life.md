# Ordinary life on the page

Constant peril numbs; a reader can only fear the loss of a life they have seen someone living. So
show ordinary competence, obligation and pleasure: work, food, money, repair, sleep, the order
someone does things in. Show it **inside a scene that wants something**, never instead of one. The
argument happens over the mending, and every domestic detail you set down is something the plot
can take.

## Examples

**Texture as a pause, so the reader waits for the story to resume:**

> They ate dinner together in the family bunk. It was a pleasant evening. Afterwards Tovi tidied up
> and her brother did his lessons.

**Texture riding along, specific and threatenable:**

> Tovi ate standing up, because the second chair still had the broken leg she'd been meaning to
> weld for a month. Her brother had his lessons spread over the good one. "Day thirty-one," he
> said, not looking up, "is that a school day?"

Nine words of chair carry money, character and mood, and the question underneath is the plot. When
the bunk is lost later, the reader will remember the chair.

**Recurring texture builds a home:** the same tea, the same walk to the canteen, the same argument
about the vent. Repetition makes a place real, and its absence register.

**Prices are specific and consistent:** if a canteen meal is two credits in one chapter, it is two
credits in the next, and what it takes from a day's pay is something a character knows by heart.

## When to break it

Once an arc, a whole chapter of ordinary life earns its place: after a heavy loss, while the cost
is being absorbed, or just before the climax, where its calm reads as dread. It still has a want,
a turn and a cost; the texture is the medium, not the point.
